## 📌 Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

- The strategy space is **not** a list of four unrelated ideas. It is one axis: **stick ↔ carrot**, ordered by how hard the instrument presses. Dissuasion and prohibition are *quantity* instruments; tolls and congestion charging are *price* instruments; bike lanes, transit and carpooling are the *carrot* side.
- **The decisive finding in the current literature is counter-intuitive:** in Milan, restricting *vehicle types* changed mode choice **more** than a **50 % charge increase** (Moulin & Urbano 2025). "Just raise the price" is not the strongest lever available.
- **Price instruments lose power over time.** OECD/ITF (2018) finds Swedish congestion-charge elasticities *declining* because the most price-sensitive traffic was already priced off the road. Evidence of "drivers just pay" is therefore evidence about the *residual*, not about the instrument.
- **Licence-plate rationing ("even-odd") is contested, not settled.** Davis (2008, *JPE*) found no air-quality gain and a ~20 % fleet increase in Mexico City; a 2021 *Transport Reviews* re-review **challenges** the magnitude of the second-car effect. Writing "affluent families bought a second car" as fact is an unsupported claim.
- **"Singapore is the only country that succeeded" is factually wrong in 2026.** Singapore is a city-state, and London (2003), Stockholm (2007), Gothenburg (2013) and Milan (2012) all operate pricing schemes with evaluated traffic effects. The sentence is a 2000s-era claim that has been overtaken.
- **The real constraint on prohibition is political, not technical.** Support for pedestrianisation *rises after implementation*, and the strongest predictor of opposition is **car ownership plus habitual car use** (Brussels). You are not arguing with evidence; you are arguing with a sunk cost.

## 🧠 Detailed Analysis

### 1. Theoretical framework — one axis, not four categories

The passage that prompted this note presents four "strategies". They are better read as points on two dimensions:

| Instrument | Type | What it rations | Who bears the cost | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Speed bumps, parking-supply caps | quantity (soft) | convenience | everyone, diffusely | too weak to change mode choice |
| Downtown ban | quantity (hard) | access | non-car users gain, drivers lose | displacement to boundary roads |
| Plate-number rationing | quantity (hard) | access by day | households with one car | **second-car purchase** |
| Tolls / congestion charge | price | access | whoever values the trip least | **residual demand** + equity |
| Ownership quota (COE) | quantity on stock | the right to own | buyers of vehicles | **wealth filter** |
| Bike lanes, transit, carpooling | carrot | — | public budget | useless without the stick |

**Theory that does the work here:**

- **Pigouvian taxation.** Congestion and emissions are negative externalities; a charge equal to marginal external cost internalises them. This is why Milan's scheme began life in 2008 as a *pollution* charge (Ecopass) and was converted to a *congestion* charge (Area C) in 2012.
- **Price vs quantity (Weitzman 1974).** A regulator who does not know the marginal abatement cost should prefer **quantity** instruments when the damage curve is steep. Plate rationing is a quantity instrument. That is a real theoretical justification — and it explains why cities reach for it, even though the empirics are poor.
- **Elasticity heterogeneity.** The instrument does not act on "drivers"; it acts on the *subset* whose trips are discretionary. This single idea explains almost every confusing result in this literature.
- **Induced demand / triple convergence.** New road capacity is consumed by (i) route shifts, (ii) time shifts, (iii) mode shifts. This is the mechanism behind the original passage's closing claim that congestion is a "countervailing force".

**Honest gap:** the "dissuasion via reduced parking supply" claim from the original passage has **no source in this inventory**. ### 3. Case studies

#### Case 1 — Mexico City: Hoy No Circula (1989–)
- **Situation:** record pollution; a weekday ban by last plate digit, 05:00–22:00, near-universal compliance because detection was cheap and fines high.
- **Action:** quantity rationing of access, one day per week per vehicle.
- **Result:** no measurable air-quality gain; **fleet +~20 %**, skewed toward older, dirtier second cars; estimated cost >\$300M/yr. Saturday extension (2008) also failed, with substitution toward taxi/Uber.
- **What makes it the canonical failure:** it converted a car into a **driving permit**, so the rational household response was to buy a second permit.
- **Where the case is weaker than usually told:** the 2021 *Transport Reviews* re-review disputes the second-car magnitude; Guerra & Millard-Ball find metro-wide registrations no faster than the national trend. **The direction is well supported; the size is contested.** Cite it as "the most-cited studies find", not as settled.

#### Case 2 — Singapore: Vehicle Quota System + COE (1990–)
- **Situation:** land-scarce city-state; roads are 12 % of land area — the policy is a land-use constraint before it is a transport one.
- **Action:** open auction for a 10-year Certificate of Entitlement; growth rate set to **0 %** for cars and motorcycles since Feb 2018 (0.25 % for goods vehicles/buses), maintained to Jan 2028; quota tied to rolling deregistrations.
- **Result:** growth in the fleet is a **policy variable**, set annually, not an outcome. That is the actual achievement — not "low traffic" but **controllability**.
- **What it costs:** the COE is a **wealth filter**. Lee & Koh raised the equity objection in 1994 and it has never been answered, only accepted. Any honest treatment must present Singapore as *effective and regressive*, not as a model to copy wholesale.

#### Case 3 — London / Stockholm / Gothenburg / Milan: price instruments
- London (2003, 20.7 km²), Stockholm (2007, ~30 km², after a 6-month trial), Gothenburg (2013, a small city of ~500k), Milan (Ecopass 2008 → Area C 2012).
- **Consistent results:** traffic down inside the cordon, emissions −13 to −18 %, collisions down sharply, welfare positive in CBA, distribution **regressive before revenue recycling**.
- **Milan is the important one for this note:** the 2025 evaluation finds **vehicle-type restriction > 50 % price rise** in effect on mode choice. If that generalises, the policy conversation has over-invested in price and under-invested in composition.

#### Case 4 — London LTNs / Brussels pedestrianisation: the acceptability case
- LTNs: −6 % driving, ~−1.3 km/driver/day, no ownership change, possible boundary displacement, no detectable harm to high-street retail activity (BT/Mastercard card data, 2023).
- Brussels central boulevards: support **rose after** implementation; opponents were disproportionately car-owning habitual drivers.
- **Implication:** the political fight is front-loaded. Evaluations conducted at t+6 months measure anger, not outcome.

### 4. Business-model and policy-design implications

1. **Revenue recycling is not a detail — it is the equity mechanism.** Ticket the regressivity of the charge; solve it on the expenditure side. Gothenburg's CBA only works as a welfare story once you say where the money goes.
2. **Composition beats level.** If Milan generalises, the highest-return instrument is not "higher toll" but "cleaner fleet allowed in" — which is also cheaper politically because it targets vehicles, not people.
3. **Expect elasticity decay and plan the next increment.** A charge introduced at t₀ will have less effect at t₀+5 for the same real value. Index it, or accept a one-off.
4. **Design for the marginal user, not the average one.** Price instruments act on discretionary trips. If your city's trips are mostly non-discretionary (commuting with no alternative), the instrument will look like it "does nothing" — because it is acting on a subset you cannot see in the aggregate.
5. **Any quantity instrument must close the substitution channel.** Plate rationing failed because a second car was available. A congestion charge cannot be "bought around" the same way, which is a genuine advantage of price over quantity *here* — the opposite of the textbook Weitzman intuition, and worth understanding.

### 6. Method note — how to read a secondary source like the one that started this

The passage this note was built from is a **secondary source with one citation (`[1]`, attached to the Singapore sentence)**. It mixes:
- **strong evidence** (pricing) with
- **weak evidence** (dissuasion) with
- **a false claim** (Singapore as "the only country")
- **a contested claim stated as fact** (second-car purchase)

Three transferable rules:

1. **A citation attached to one sentence does not cover the paragraph.** Check what `[1]` actually supports before inheriting the paragraph's authority.
2. **A claim of uniqueness ("the only country") is a claim about the state of the world at a date.** It is the easiest kind of claim to falsify and the most likely to have rotted.
3. **"Most evidence shows X" with no list is not evidence.** Ask for the list, then tier it.

**Terminology map (English → precise English), because the sloppiness is in the source:**

| Source wording | Precise term | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| "dissuasion" | *soft quantity rationing* (traffic calming, parking caps) | it is not a ban; the distinction drives the whole taxonomy |
| "toll" | *road-user charge* (cordon/area) vs *parking levy* | different incidence, different legal basis |
| "congestion pricing" | *Pigouvian cordon charge*, often time-varying | time-variance is what produces intertemporal substitution |
| "modal choice" | *mode choice* | "mode" here is transport mode, not a system state |
| "countervailing forces" | *induced demand* / *triple convergence* | it has a mechanism and a literature; use them |
| "intermodal transport" | *combined/multimodal transport* | the source sentence is missing a word ("individual **and public** transport") |

## 📝 Research Journey

- **Why:** it started as a translation task — "explain this requirement in Vietnamese, indirectly" — and became a lesson in how much of a plausible-sounding policy paragraph evaporates when each sentence is asked for its source.
- **Struggle:** the hardest part was not finding sources; it was discovering that the *most confident* sentence in the passage (Singapore, "the only country") is the one that is plainly wrong, while the *most hedged* sentence (congestion as a countervailing force) is the one with the strongest theoretical backing. Hedging and accuracy are inversely correlated in secondary sources.
- **Aha moment:** Milan 2025 — a **vehicle-type restriction beat a 50 % price increase**. I had absorbed the standard economics prior that price is the efficient instrument. Price is efficient *when the substitute is elastic and unpriced*. When the substitute is "a different car in the same garage", restricting composition dominates. Elasticity is not a property of the good; it is a property of the alternative.
- **Career link:** this is the same reasoning an AI engineer uses when choosing between a **price** intervention (a threshold, a rate limit, a cost budget) and a **quantity** intervention (a hard cap, an allow-list). The transport literature has 40 years of evidence on which fails where — that is free engineering judgement, imported.
