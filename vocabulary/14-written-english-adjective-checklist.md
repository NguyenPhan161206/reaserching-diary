## 📌 Term List & Quick Reference

### The one rule that makes the other 40 unnecessary

> **An adjective is an opinion. A verb is evidence.**

Everything below is an application of that rule. If a draft sentence can be re-expressed as *a subject, a verb, and an object*, it will improve — and you will not need to remember a single adjective. Checklists of "bad words" fail because they produce *substitution* (replacing *hard-working* with *diligent*) instead of *restructuring*. Substitution gives you a synonym with the same unverifiable content.

| You wrote | Why it is weak | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| *"I am **hard-working** and **responsible**."* | two unverifiable opinions, zero evidence | *"I owned the release checklist and shipped 14 releases in a quarter."* |
| *"I am **creative**."* | unfalsifiable, and the most common CV adjective | *"I redesigned the onboarding flow; activation rose from 31% to 44%."* |
| *"I am a **fast learner**."* | unverifiable, and *fast* has a technical meaning here | *"I learned the deployment stack in three weeks and shipped my first change unassisted."* |
| *"I worked on the **AI** project."* | *worked on* is a participation claim | *"I implemented the retrieval layer and reduced p95 latency by 38%."* |
| *"I helped the team **improve efficiency**."* | unattributed, unmeasured | *"I automated the reporting job, cutting 6 hours/week of manual work."* |

### The 8 highest-risk adjectives — the short list

If you only audit eight things before submitting anything, audit these.

| # | Adjective | The specific damage | Use instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **passionate** | the single most discounted word in a tech CV | a verb: *"I teach myself …"* |
| 2 | **hard-working** | unverifiable; also one of the traits whose *teams* underperform (§4) | evidence of output volume |
| 3 | **creative** | means nothing without an object | name the thing you created |
| 4 | **responsible** | read as *liability* in the R+1 sense: *"responsible for…"* = *supervises* | *"I own…"* — active, and unambiguous |
| 5 | **detail-oriented** | a cliché; also **lexically wrong for the meaning** | *"I checked the edge cases"* or a number |
| 6 | **team player** | unfalsifiable *and* a cliché | *"I ran the weekly sync and wrote the handover doc"* |
| 7 | **self-motivated** | implies you needed no resources or support — it is not a compliment | *"I shipped … without a spec"* |
| 8 | **think outside the box** | ❌ a **verb phrase in adjectival position**; one of the most-copied phrasings on the internet | *"I proposed the caching change that replaced the design-doc approach"* |

## 🧠 Detailed Vocabulary Analysis

### 0. Why written English is unforgiving about adjectives, and speaking is not

This is the asymmetry that makes this note necessary, and it comes from how the two modalities differ mechanically.

| | **Spoken** | **Written (CV / post / email)** |
|---|---|---|
| The reader is | a single person, live, already tracking your meaning | a stranger, scanning, with an incentive to disbelieve you |
| Feedback | immediate — they frown | none — the sentence stands alone for months |
| Repetition penalty | low — you naturally paraphrase while speaking | ⚠️ high — the same adjective three times in a page reads as a vocabulary failure |
| The reader knows your intent | ✅ tone and gesture carry it | ❌ only the words carry it |
| **What the reader is actually judging** | whether you're easy to talk to | whether your claims are **verifiable** |

💡 **The load-bearing insight:** in writing, the reader has already decided, before your adjectives, that you are claiming to be good. Your adjectives are not adding information — they are **asking for trust**. That is a very different rhetorical job, and it is why the same sentence works in an interview and fails in a CV:

- ✅ **Interview:** *"I'm quite hard-working — I usually pick up the tasks nobody else wants."*
- ❌ **CV:** *"Hard-working team player who takes initiative."* — because the reader can neither verify it nor ask you about it.
- ✅ **CV:** *"Took ownership of the deployment pipeline and documented it, which is now the team default."*

⚠️ **And the second asymmetry, which is about repetition:** in speech, avoiding repeated words is a *fluency* concern (Band 7 vocabulary is partly "avoid repetition"). In a written CV, it is a **credibility** concern — three paragraphs of self-praise without a single number reads as someone who has nothing measurable to say, and readers interpret that as exactly that.

### 1. The pre-emption table — never deny a negative label, state the behaviour

[[vocabulary/13-personality-traits-neutral-positive-negative.md]] §6.2 established that `lazy` is the most dangerous label on a whiteboard because it **asserts a motive** where the situation deserves the blame. Written self-promotion has the same structure: readers bring labels, and you can either *deny* them or *prevent* them.

| Reader's likely label | ❌ Denial (creates the problem) | ✅ Pre-emption (removes it) |
|---|---|---|
| *lazy / passive* | *"I am **not lazy**"* | *"I shipped the fix without being assigned it."* |
| *unmotivated* | *"I am **very self-motivated**"* | *"I read the codebase over two weekends to land my first PR."* |
| *arrogant* | *"I am **humble**"* | *"I pair with teammates; my last PR rewrote a senior engineer's design."* |
| *not a team player* | *"I am an excellent **team player**"* | *"I wrote the handover doc the next two interns still use."* |
| *unfocused* | *"I am **detail-oriented**"* | *"I found the timezone bug in a flow that had passed QA for months."* |
| *no impact* | *"I improved efficiency"* | *"Cut the pipeline from 40 to 6 minutes."* |

⚠️ **Why denial backfires, mechanically:** a denial is a *topical prime*. Saying *"I'm not lazy"* puts *lazy* in the reader's working memory with a claim attached to it — you have now both introduced the word and demonstrated that you thought about it, which implies you had a reason to. **State the behaviour; the label never gets raised.**

### 2. Register ladders — the same trait at five levels of formality

The trait is not the problem; the *cost* of each word at each level is.

| Register | How to sound like yourself | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **CV bullet — too plain** | *good* | *"Good team player"* |
| **CV bullet — the trap** | adjective about yourself | *"I am a hard-working, responsible and creative person."* |
| **CV bullet — target** | **subject + verb + object + number** | *"Rebuilt the deployment pipeline; release time fell from 40 to 6 minutes."* |
| **LinkedIn post** | trait + a concrete scene | *"I'm usually the person who asks the awkward question in design review — last month that's what caught the migration bug."* |
| **Formal email** | professional frame, no self-adjective | *"Happy to take this on"* ✅ / *"I am a reliable and responsible team member"* ❌ |
| **Cover letter** | one trait + a professional frame | *"I flag risk early rather than late — see the rollback plan I added before the v2 launch."* |
| **Interview** | trait + contrast | *"I'm quite analytical, though open-minded — I changed my whole approach after the first user test failed."* |
| **Peer review / reference** | trait + comparison | *"She's the most dependable person on the team; she never dropped a commitment."* |

⚠️ **The email row is the one learners miss.** A formal email is *less* personal than a CV, yet self-adjectives there read as insecure. *"I would be happy to assist"* ✅ / *"I am a hard-working and motivated individual who would be a great fit"* ❌ — the second is the register error that makes a competent candidate read as inexperienced.

### 3. The Viet-Glish patterns, by category

Each row is the *construction* that fails, not just the word.

#### 3.1 Adjective → verb

Vietnamese adjectives are routinely used where English needs a verb clause, because the verb can be dropped in the source language.

| ❌ Viet-Glish | ✅ English | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| *"I am **responsible for** the API."* | *"I **own** the API."* · *"I **am responsible for** the API."* (also correct, but reads as supervisory — see §4) | *responsible for* is a role, not an achievement |
| *"I am **experienced in** Python."* | *"I have **built** … with Python."* | *experienced in* is a claim with no verb behind it |
| *"I am **interested in** AI."* | *"I **am working on** / **I study** AI."* | *interested in* is a state, not an activity |
| *"I am **good at** problem solving."* | *"I **solve** problems quickly"* | same |
| *"I am **familiar with** Docker."* | *"I have **containerised** two services with Docker."* | *familiar with* is the weakest of these three |

💡 **The general test:** if the sentence would still be complete if you deleted the adjective, the adjective was doing the job of a verb.

#### 3.2 Intensifiers that do not exist in English

Vietnamese intensifies freely (*rất, cực kỳ, vô cùng*). English over-intensification reads as non-native, and *very + adjective* is the single clearest marker.

| ❌ | ✅ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *"very **hard-working**"* | *"consistently ships"* · *"reliably delivers"* | *very* + trait adjective = beginner marker |
| *"very **responsible**"* | *"owns the outcome"* | verb beats adverb + adjective |
| *"very **creative**"* | name the artefact | *creative* needs an object |
| *"extremely **good**"* | *"cut costs by 20%"* | evaluative words need evidence |
| *"**so** flexible"* | *"I rescheduled three times to meet deadlines"* | |
| *"**really** passionate about"* | *"I have rebuilt this project four times"* | |

⚠️ Note that *very* is not forbidden — *"This is very useful"* is fine. The problem is specific: **`very` + a personality adjective** is where it clusters, because those are exactly the adjectives you are using as a substitute for a fact.

#### 3.3 The over-formal register leak

Vietnamese formal writing leans on nominalisation and Latinate vocabulary; professional English rewards plainness.

| ❌ Over-formal | ✅ Plain | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *"I made a **utilization** of…"* | *"I used…"* | *utilisation* is a false friend — it reads as *using up*, and `use` is the word |
| *"I did an **accomplishment** of…"* | *"I completed…"* | noun-as-verb leak; very common in Asian-academic English |
| *"Please **kindly** grant me the approval"* | *"Could you approve this?"* | *kindly* is not a politeness intensifier |
| *"I would like to **thank** you for your **consideration**"* | *"Thanks for reviewing this."* | the closing formula of a business letter, unnecessary in email |
| *"in order to facilitate the **optimisation** of…"* | *"to improve…"* | nominalisation + Latinate |
| *"**Regarding** the above-mentioned issue"* | *"On this issue"* / *"About"* | *above-mentioned* is 1970s |
| *"I would like to **inform** you that…"* | *"Just a note: …"* | |
| *"Thank you for your **time and **consideration**."* | *"Thanks for the review."* | |

💡 **A useful heuristic:** in professional English, if a word ends in `-tion`, `-ment`, or `-ance` *and* a shorter word exists, the shorter word is usually right. This is not a rule — *"an implementation"* is correct — but it catches the majority of register leaks.

### 4. The words that carry a *different meaning* in a CV

These are worse than the cliché adjectives, because the cliché is at least recognisable as empty. These are **accurate-looking and semantically wrong**.

| Word | What you mean | What the reader hears | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **responsible** | I did the work | ⚠️ **I supervised others** — the "responsible for" reading in a team context | *"I own"* / *"I built"* / *"I delivered"* |
| **detail-oriented** | careful with small things | ⚠️ **obsessive about trivia** — can read as a criticism of *focus* | name the specific check you did |
| **ambitious** | I want to grow | ambiguous — ambition can be read as *for myself*, i.e. *what can you give me?* | *"I led the migration…"* — let the record show the ambition |
| **modest** | humble | ❌ **low income** in `modest salary` / `modest means` | never *modest* about money in English |
| **naive** | open-hearted | ❌ **foolish / inexperienced** — an insult in English | *"open to new approaches"* |
| **crazy** | extreme | ❌ reads as unprofessional, or as a mental-health slur | *"unusually aggressive"* / *"a bold bet"* |
| **sensitive** | empathetic | ❌ **easily offended** | *"empathetic"* / *"attuned"* |
| **emotional** | passionate | ❌ **unable to stay calm** | *"engaged"* / *"committed"* |
| **loyal** | committed | fine as a value, ❌ weak as a claim — a loyalty claim implies you might leave | *"I stayed 3 years"* |
| **flexible** | adaptable | ⚠️ in HR reading, often *"will accept bad conditions"* | *"I re-scoped three milestones when requirements changed"* |
| **self-motivated** | driven | ⚠️ *"needed no support"* — reads as unable to work with others | *"I initiated …"* |
| **think outside the box** | creative | ❌ not even a grammatical adjective | name the unconventional thing you did |

⚠️ **The two most dangerous for a Vietnamese speaker, because the Vietnamese words are neutral or positive:**

- **`cẩn thận`** (careful) → `careful` ✅; but **`chi tiết`** (detail) as in *detail-oriented* ❌ is the classic. In Vietnamese *"tỉ mỉn"* is praise; in English *"fussy"* is the same word's meaning in the wrong register.
- **`thẳng thắn`** (frank, straightforward) → *"thẳng thắn"* maps to `blunt`, which English reserves for *feedback* and reads as *rude* about a person. The correct register word is **`candid`** or **`forthright`** — see note 13 §4.4, where this exact three-way (candour / bluntness / rudeness) is worked out in English.

### 5. Sentence-level rewrites — the same six claims, seven ways

This is the part to actually copy. All six are claims a student CV makes constantly.

| Register | Sentence |
|---|---|
| ❌ Weak | *"I am a hard-working and responsible student who is very passionate about AI."* |
| ⚠️ Better | *"A hard-working, responsible student passionate about AI."* (fragments still, but grammatical — the least-bad of the adjective options) |
| ✅ **Plain and safe** | *"Final-year Computer Science student. I built three deployed projects and wrote about what I learned."* |
| ✅ **Verb + evidence** | *"I built and deployed three projects end to end, from data ingestion to the API layer."* |
| ✅ **Verb + number** | *"Shipped 3 deployed projects; my retrieval pipeline serves 200 queries a day at p95 under 400 ms."* |
| ✅ **Verb + constraint** | *"Took ownership of the data pipeline when no spec existed, and wrote the spec myself."* |
| ✅ **Verb + contrast** | *"I am more interested in the parts of AI that fail than in the demos — which is why I wrote my own eval harness."* |

⚠️ **Notice what the last one does.** *"More interested in the parts that fail than in the demos"* is a **character statement** — it signals seriousness, self-awareness and a preference for engineering over hype — and it contains **no self-adjective at all**. This is the move: **convey the trait through the choice of what to talk about.** A hiring manager who reads *"I reimplemented the evaluation harness to find out whether my own results were real"* concludes *rigorous, honest, self-starting* without a single adjective, and can verify any of it.

### 6. The formats that are exempt — where adjectives are correct

⚠️ **A checklist that says "never use adjectives" is a bad checklist**, because there are places where they are the right tool.

| Format | Adjectives? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| CV bullet | ❌ no | scanned, adversarial reader, unverifiable |
| LinkedIn post about a *decision* | ⚠️ sparingly | the post is itself the evidence; a trait can be the *subject* (*"The least glamorous part of my week…"*) |
| **LinkedIn / CV "About" / summary** | ✅ **yes** | ⚠️ this is the **one place** self-adjectives belong — it is a prose paragraph, not a scanned bullet list, and "About" sections are written in first person by convention |
| Cover letter opening | ✅ yes, one | *"I am applying because…"* is a paragraph, and one well-chosen adjective is fine |
| Interview | ✅ yes, many | the reader is live and wants to know how you describe yourself |
| Peer review / reference for someone else | ✅ yes, many | you are the evidence, not the claimant |
| Team charter / design doc | ✅ yes | *"a service that is reliable and boring"* is a legitimate engineering position |
| **Skills section** | ✅ yes, **but as labels** | *"Python, FastAPI, PostgreSQL, Docker"* — here adjectives would be noise; a *self-assessed level* (*"advanced"*) is the thing to avoid |

💡 **The summary-section exception explains why CV advice feels contradictory.** "Never use adjectives on a CV" is wrong for the *About* section and right for the *bullets*, because the two have different readers and different jobs. What unites them is the rule, not the format: **an adjective is fine when you are describing a stance, an environment, or a value — and a liability when you are asking to be believed about yourself without evidence.** *"I want to work on problems that matter"* is a value statement (fine). *"I am a results-driven professional"* is a belief request (not fine).

### 7. The self-check before you submit

Seven questions, in order. This is the checklist to actually run.

1. **Delete every self-adjective.** Does anything still prove the claim? If not, the evidence is missing, not the adjective.
2. **Does every claim have a verb?** Search for `responsible for`, `experienced in`, `familiar with`, `interested in`, `good at`, `helped with` — these are all hiding a missing verb clause.
3. **Search for `very`, `extremely`, `really`, `so`, `highly`.** Each one before a personality adjective is a tell.
4. **Check the eight high-risk words** from the top of this note, and the twelve meaning-shifts in §4.
5. **Find your strongest number** and put it in the top third of the document. If you have no numbers, that is a content problem, not a writing problem.
6. **Read the first bullet aloud.** If it sounds like a cover letter rather than a person who built something, rewrite it as *I did X, measured by Y*.
7. **Ask the counterfactual:** *"how would I know this is true?"* — if the answer is *"because I wrote it"*, the claim is unverified and the adjective is doing the writer's job.

## 📝 Research Journey

- **Why:** This note is the §6.6 register ladder from [[vocabulary/13-personality-traits-neutral-positive-negative.md]] turned into something usable *at the moment of writing*. A register ladder in a vocabulary note is theory; a checklist with a search list is a tool. The trigger was concrete — the LinkedIn evaluation session ([[personal_evaluations/05-linkedin-research-diary-post-eval.md]]) had already established *"writing is not speaking"* and that my adjectives carried more cost in a post than in a conversation, and the practical follow-up was to work out exactly which ones and why.
- **Struggle:** The main difficulty was **resisting the substitution instinct.** My first instinct on every bad word was to find a better word — and every better word was exactly as unverifiable. `passionate` → `enthusiastic` → `ardent` is a three-step improvement to *nothing*. The insight only arrived when I asked *what job is the adjective doing in this sentence*, and realised it is doing the writer's job: supplying evidence that does not exist. That reframing is what turned a word list into a structural rule. The second difficulty was **not over-applying the rule.** "Never use adjectives" would have broken the *About* section, the design-doc register, and every interview answer — so the note has to carry an explicit exemption section (§6), which is longer than it should be and still not complete.
- **Aha moment:** §5's last row. Writing *"I reimplemented the evaluation harness to find out whether my own results were real"* **conveys** rigorous, honest and self-starting without **claiming** any of them. That is strictly better than the best adjective because it is also *verifiable* — and it generalises: **choose what to talk about so that the choice itself is the evidence.** The second aha came from [[psychology_society/04-big-five-personality-model.md]]: high average conscientiousness is associated with *lower* team performance, which means filling a CV with conscientiousness adjectives is not even self-serving — it may be signalling a liability that a good team needs to offset.
- **Career link:** This is the note with the shortest path to a changed document. The CV is [[cv/01-master-profile.md]], and a LaTeX CV cannot be fixed by a word choice — it has to be fixed by finding measurable claims, which is a **data collection task**, not a writing task. Doing this note first makes that task concrete: I now know exactly which claims to go and measure. The second link is portfolio-facing — a LinkedIn post and a GitHub README are the two public documents that get read, and both are written English, where this checklist applies in full.

## 🤔 Open Questions

1. **Is there evidence on whether self-descriptive adjectives help or hurt applications at all?** §1 and §3.1 argue they are weak signals on credibility grounds, which is a rhetorical argument, not an empirical one. Does recruiter-behaviour research exist, or is this folklore that happens to be true?
2. **How much does the *About* section actually matter?** §6 exempts it on the grounds that it is first-person prose by convention. Is there any evidence that a strong *About* paragraph improves outcomes, or is it simply where people expect to find adjectives and therefore tolerate them?
3. **What is the correct English for *tỉ mỉn*?** §4 flags it as an untranslated trap — *careful* ✅, *meticulous* ✅, *fussy* ❌ — but Vietnamese uses *tỉ mỉn* for both careful work and excessive fussiness depending on context, and English splits those into different words. Is *meticulous* positive in a CV context, or does it read as the *fussy* pole?
4. **Does the checklist survive contact with a non-native recruiter?** Every judgement here is about how a *native* reader reacts. Some of these words carry different weight outside the US/UK professional register — `ambitious` and `loyal` in particular have different readings in hiring contexts across markets. Worth checking before treating the list as universal.
5. **How much should an applicant accommodate a reader's expectations that they cannot verify?** There is a counter-argument to §5: in some markets and seniorities, a CV with no self-description at all reads as *arrogant* or *too junior*. Is there a calibrated amount of self-characterisation, and who determines it?
