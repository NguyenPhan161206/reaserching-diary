## 📌 Term List & Quick Reference

This vocabulary belongs to one listening/reading passage — a city trying to **push cars out of its centre** while preserving an **older, denser urban fabric** (the *souk*), and then a museum or exhibition on **modern architecture** where designers *embrace* a new style, *expose* the structure, and *capture* light. The words group into two clusters:

### Cluster A — the city and its traffic

| **resistance** | `/rɪˈzɪstəns/` | refusal to accept a change; physical opposition | general · academic |

| **monitoring** | `/ˈmɑːnɪtərɪŋ/` | the ongoing observation/measuring of something | tech · policy |

**Why this note exists:** the two batches are a single passage's worth of vocabulary, and they share one trap — **each word has a plain sense and a specialist sense, and the passage uses the specialist one.** *Construction* is not just "building", it is also "what you decide a text means" (*a construction of history*). *Expose* in a building does not mean "to endanger" — it means "to leave deliberately visible". *Monitor* is not a noun you watch, it is a **verb** the city does to its air quality. Getting the register right is the whole game.

## 🧠 Detailed Vocabulary Analysis

### 1. TRAFFIC FEE — `/ˈtræfɪk fiː/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Noun (policy):** a charge levied on vehicles entering a designated zone, designed to reduce congestion, cut emissions, and fund public transport.
- Synonyms and near-neighbours, in increasing order of formality: **toll** (a fee to use a road), **congestion charge / congestion pricing** (a zone-based fee — London, Stockholm, Singapore), **road pricing**, **levy** (a tax-like charge), **tariff**.
- Verb forms: **charge** a fee, **levy** a fee, **impose** a fee, **pay** a fee.

| Fee type | Where it applies | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **toll** | a specific road/bridge/tunnel | motorway tolls |
| **congestion charge** | an entire city zone | London (since 2003) |
| **parking fee** | a parking space | city-centre parking |
| **road tax / vehicle tax** | owning/using a vehicle | annual road tax |

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **impose / introduce / levy a traffic fee** · **scrap / abolish a fee** (note: *scrap*, not *delete*)
- **meet (with) resistance** from residents/drivers — see term 3
- **force cars out of the centre** — see term 9
- **revenue from the congestion charge is ring-fenced for** public transport — *ring-fence* = chốt riêng nguồn tiền cho một mục đích

#### Viet-Glish pitfall vs. Band 7.0+ alternative
- ❌ *Viet-Glish mistake:* "The government made a money for cars to go into the city."
- ✅ *Native standard:* "The city **imposed a congestion charge**, **forcing** private cars out of the centre and **funding** bus and tram upgrades with the revenue."

### 2. SOUK — `/suːk/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Noun:** an open-air or covered marketplace in North Africa and the Middle East, typically a maze of narrow lanes lined with small stalls and workshops — a *bazaar* (bazaar is the Persian/Turkish-family synonym).
- Often found in travel/history reading: "the ancient **souk** in Marrakech", "the winding lanes of the souk".
- Distinguish from **market** (generic), **bazaar** (Persian origin), **marketplace** (the open area), **mall** (modern shopping centre).

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **the narrow / winding lanes of the souk** · **a sprawling souk** · **haggle / bargain** in the souk (bargaining is the norm, not a fixed price)
- **souk** borrows into English unchanged — no article needed in generic use (*"souk traders"*), but *the souk* when specific.

### 3. RESISTANCE — `/rɪˈzɪstəns/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Noun (social/policy):** opposition to a plan or change — *meet resistance*, *face resistance*, *overcome resistance*.
- **Noun (physics/engineering):** the opposition to a force — electrical **resistance** ($R = V/I$), aerodynamic **drag** (also called resistance), and in materials **fatigue resistance**, **wear resistance**, **corrosion resistance**.
- **Noun (medicine):** **antibiotic resistance** — the same word carrying a public-health meaning.

The word is a *nominalisation* of the verb *resist*. **The structure "meet with resistance" is fixed** — you do not "get resistance" in academic prose, you **meet (with)** it or **encounter** it.

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **meet (with) / encounter / face resistance** (from X) · **overcome / wear down / break down resistance** · **strong / fierce / stiff resistance** · **pockets of resistance** (isolated remaining opposition)
- **get / build up a resistance to** (an antibiotic, a disease) — note: here *to*, and it means immunity, not opposition
- **the path of least resistance** (idiom) — làm việc dễ nhất / ngại khó

#### Viet-Glish pitfall vs. Band 7.0+ alternative
- ❌ *Viet-Glish mistake:* "The plan got a lot of resistance by the local people."
- ✅ *Native standard:* "The scheme **met with stiff resistance from local residents.**"

---

### 4. CONSTRUCTION — `/kənˈstrʌkʃən/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Noun (industry/act):** the process and business of building structures — *under construction*, *a construction site*, *the construction industry*.
- **Noun (abstract / academic — the easily-missed sense):** an interpretation built up from parts — *a social construction*, *a theoretical construction*. This sense is common in IELTS Reading and in the social sciences.
- **Noun (grammar):** *a grammatical construction* — a structured pattern (e.g. the passive construction).

| Use | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| physical | building work | *The road is under construction.* |
| abstract | an interpretation | *Race is a social construction.* |
| grammatical | a structure | *the causative construction* |

#### Word family
- **construct** (v) — to build; also to form an idea
- **construction** (n) · **constructive** (adj — helpful, as of criticism) · **constructively** (adv) · **constructor** (n — one who builds; also a programming function) · **reconstruction** (n)

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **under construction** · **a construction site / firm / worker / boom** · **road / bridge / housing construction**
- **deconstruction** (philosophy) and **reconstruction** (rebuilding; also the US post-Civil-War period)

#### Viet-Glish pitfall vs. Band 7.0+ alternative
- ❌ *Viet-Glish mistake:* "The construction of this building is happening now, so it is noisy."
- ✅ *Native standard:* "The building is **under construction**, so the site is noisy." (Use the adverbial phrase, not a nominalised subject.)

### 5. MAINTENANCE — `/ˈmeɪntənəns/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Noun (technical):** the work of keeping equipment, roads, or buildings in good condition. It is **uncountable** — never *maintenances*.
- **Types that recur in engineering and IELTS:**

| Type | Trigger | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **routine / scheduled** | by calendar | annual road inspection |
| **preventive** | before failure | oil changes, resurfacing |
| **corrective / reactive** | after failure | pothole repair |
| **predictive** | by condition monitoring | sensor-triggered servicing |

- Note the nearby engineering vocabulary: **maintain** (v), **maintainable** (adj), **maintainability** (n), **upkeep** (colloquial synonym), **servicing**.

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **carry out / perform / be responsible for maintenance** · **routine / regular / ongoing maintenance** · **maintenance costs / crew / schedule / backlog**
- **road / building / aircraft / software maintenance** — the last is the DevOps sense, see the Section K block.

#### Viet-Glish pitfall vs. Band 7.0+ alternative
- ❌ *Viet-Glish mistake:* "The maintenances of the road take a long time."
- ✅ *Native standard:* "**Road maintenance takes** a long time" (uncountable) OR "**The upkeep of** the road takes a long time."

### 6. OUTSKIRT(S) — `/ˈaʊtskɜːrts/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Noun:** the outer parts of a town or city, farthest from the centre. **Almost always plural** — *on the outskirts of*.
- The urban-geography ladder, from centre outward: **city centre / downtown** → **inner city** → **suburbs** → **the outskirts** → **the commuter belt** → **rural fringe**. The exact meanings differ by country — in the UK, *suburb* often implies comfortable residential; *inner city* often implies older and denser, and in US usage *inner city* can carry socioeconomic connotation.

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **on the outskirts of {the city, town, London}** — note the preposition **on**, never *in*
- **the outskirts of town** · **a house on the outskirts**
- Related: **periphery** (formal, also used figuratively for "edge of a field/idea"), **fringe**, **hinterland** (the area served by a city)

#### Viet-Glish pitfall vs. Band 7.0+ alternative
- ❌ *Viet-Glish mistake:* "My family lives in the outskirt of the city."
- ✅ *Native standard:* "My family lives **on the outskirts of** the city." (Plural + *on*.)

### 7. EXHAUST FUMES — `/ɪɡˈzɔːst fjuːmz/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Noun phrase (environmental):** the mixture of gases and fine particles emitted by an internal-combustion engine. *Exhaust* = the waste gas itself or the pipe that emits it; *fumes* = the visible/smelled vapour.
- **Fumes** is always plural in this sense and stays close to *exhaust, smoke, vapour, emissions, particulates* — the pollution family.
- This phrase is a **fixed collocation**: you say *exhaust fumes*, not *exhaust gas smells* or *fume of exhaust*. The technical register prefers **exhaust emissions** or **tailpipe emissions**; the everyday/IELTS register uses **exhaust fumes**.

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **belch / spew (out) exhaust fumes** (vivid, journalistic) · **cut / reduce / curb emissions** · **inhale / be exposed to fumes** · **noxious / toxic fumes**
- Related devil vocabulary: **smog** (smoke + fog), **particulate matter (PM2.5/PM10)**, **carbon monoxide**, **NOx**, **greenhouse gases**

#### Cross-domain hook
The environmental case is the *reason* a traffic fee exists (term 1): cities price the right to emit, so that the **external cost** of fumes is borne by the driver rather than the pedestrian — this is the textbook **negative externality** and **Pigouvian tax** (see §Cross-Domain Bridge).

### 8. PEDESTRIAN(S) — `/pəˈdestriənz/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Noun:** a person travelling on foot, especially in a city/on a road. Route: Latin *pedester* (on foot) ← *pes, pedis* = foot — the same root as **pedal, pedicure, pedometer, centipede, impede**.
- **Adjective:** *pedestrian* also means "dull, uninspired" — *a pedestrian style of writing*. This is a second, easy-to-miss sense (and an IELTS lexicon point: a *pedestrian* argument is a boring one).

#### Word family & urban vocabulary
- **pedestrianise** (v — to close a street to vehicles) · **pedestrianisation** (n) · **pedestrian crossing** (vạch sang đường) · **pedestrian zone / precinct** (khu vực dành cho người đi bộ) · **footpath / pavement (BrE) / sidewalk (AmE)**
- Antonym context: **motorist / driver**, **cyclist**, **commuter**.

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **a pedestrian crossing / bridge / precinct / zone** · **pedestrian traffic** · **yield to pedestrians** (AmE traffic law) · **a pedestrian was struck / knocked down**
- **pedestrianise the city centre** — exactly what a traffic fee is meant to encourage

### 9. FORCE — `/fɔːrs/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Verb:** to make someone/something do something or move, by strength, pressure, or necessity — *force cars out*, *force residents to sell*, *be forced to leave*.
- **Noun:** physical power; also **a force** in physics ($F = ma$); also **a driving force** (nguồn động lực) and **a force for change**.
- In the urban passage, *force* is the **policy verb** that pairs with *resistance*: the city *forces* cars out, drivers *resist*.

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **force sb/sth {out of, into, to do sth}** · **be forced to** + verb · **force the pace** · **force a decision**
- **the driving force behind** (the chief cause) · **a force to be reckoned with** (idiom — đối thủ đáng gờm)
- Memory hook: **force / forcible / forcibly / enforcement** — the last is what a *fee* becomes when you refuse to pay it.

#### Viet-Glish pitfall vs. Band 7.0+ alternative
- ❌ *Viet-Glish mistake:* "The government forces the people to use bus."
- ✅ *Native standard:* "The policy **pressures / encourages** commuters to use the bus" — reserve *force* for genuine coercion; a fee *pushes* rather than *forces* choice.

---

### 10. AFTERWARDS — `/ˈæftərwərdz/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Adverb:** at a later time, after an event just mentioned. BrE spelling **afterwards**, AmE prefers **afterward**; both are widely accepted.
- It is a **discourse/time-sequencing** word, the twin of *beforehand*. In a listening passage it signals "the next step in the sequence" and is a common distractor/answer marker.

#### Native usage table

| Word | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **afterwards / afterward** | after something else | *We toured the old town, and afterwards we had tea.* |
| **after** | preposition/conjunction, needs an object | *after the tour* · *after we toured* |
| **later** | at a later time, less tied to a specific event | *I'll explain later.* |
| **subsequently** | formal/written | *Subsequently, the council banned cars.* |
| **thereafter** | very formal, written only | *Thereafter, no vehicles entered the centre.* |

- ❌ *"Afterwards, I go"* mixture of tenses — the adverb is timeline-neutral, but keep the tense consistent.
- ✅ *"Afterwards, we went for a walk"* / *"Afterwards, we discussed the exhibition."*

### 11. EXHIBITION — `/ˌeksɪˈbɪʃən/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Noun (culture):** a public display of art, science, or objects — *an art exhibition*, *a museum exhibition*, *a retrospective exhibition* (career-wide).
- **Verb choice matters:** you **hold / stage / put on / mount** an exhibition (organiser's view); you **visit / attend / see / go to** an exhibition (visitor's view). *Mount* and *stage* are the formal/curatorial picks.

#### Word family & confusables
| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **exhibition** | a public show (art, objects) |
| **exposition** | a large public show/expo; also a written explanation |
| **exhibit** | (n) a single displayed item; (v) to display |
| **exhibitor** | a person/company showing at an event |
| **exhibitionist** | ⚠ an unrelated word with an entirely different, negative meaning — do not confuse |

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **hold / stage / mount / put on an exhibition** · **an exhibition opens / runs until / closes** · **a touring / travelling exhibition** · **exhibition space / hall / catalogue**

### 12. EMBRACE — `/ɪmˈbreɪs/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Verb (literal):** to hold someone closely in the arms (ôm).
- **Verb (figurative — the passage's use):** to **accept willingly and enthusiastically** an idea, technology, or change — *embrace modernism*, *embrace digital tools*, *embrace change*. It carries approval; the opposite is **reject / resist / spurn / shun**.
- **Noun:** an embrace = a hug.

The figurative sense is what makes *embrace* a Band 7+ verb: it is warmer and more deliberate than *accept*, because it implies actively choosing something new.

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **embrace {change, technology, innovation, modernism, diversity, uncertainty}** · **fully / wholeheartedly embrace**
- **an embrace of** + noun (*his embrace of Modernist principles*)
- Contrast set for precise writing: **accept** (neutral) · **adopt** (take up for use) · **embrace** (warm, active) · **endorse** (publicly support)

#### Viet-Glish pitfall vs. Band 7.0+ alternative
- ❌ *Viet-Glish mistake:* "The architects embraced to use flat roofs."
- ✅ *Native standard:* "The architects **embraced flat roofs**" (embrace takes a bare noun/phrase, not *to* + verb).

### 13. CAPTURE — `/ˈkæptʃər/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Verb (literal):** to catch and hold a person/animal; to take by force.
- **Verb (art/tech — the passage's use):** to **record or reproduce faithfully** — a photograph *captures* light; a painting *captures* a mood; a sensor *captures* data; a note *captures* an idea.
- **Verb (economics — a distinct sense):** to **gain** a share of value — *capture value*, *capture market share* (see §Cross-Domain Bridge).

| Sense | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| physical | seize, catch | *capture the suspect* |
| representational | record faithfully | *capture light on the facade* |
| commercial | win/gain | *capture 30% of the market* |
| data | record into a system | *capture the reading* |

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **capture {light, a moment, the mood, attention, the essence}** · **capture {data, an image, a signal}** · **capture {value, market share, demand}** · **capture the imagination** (inspire people)
- Noun forms: **capture** (n — *the capture of the city*; *data capture*), **captivation** (rare), **captivating** (adj — hấp dẫn mê hoặc)

### 14. EXPOSE — `/ɪkˈspoʊz/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Verb (general):** to make visible something normally hidden or protected — *expose the truth*, *expose sb to danger*.
- **Verb (architecture/design — the passage's use):** to **leave a structural or functional element deliberately visible** rather than concealing it — *exposed brick*, *exposed concrete*, *exposed beams*. This is a defining move of Modernism and of *industrial-style* interiors.

| Sense | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| reveal | make known | *expose corruption* |
| endanger | subject to risk | *expose workers to fumes* |
| architectural | leave visible | *exposed brick walls* |

#### Word family & collocations
- **expose / exposure / exposed / exposé** (n — a revealing report)
- **expose {the structure, the brickwork, the concrete, the truth, a scandal}** · **leave sth exposed** · **be exposed to {risk, fumes, sunlight}**
- The architectural sense connects directly to *pure* and *purism* (terms 16–17): purism wants the structure *exposed and unadorned*, not dressed in ornament.

### 15. ROOF — `/ruːf/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Noun:** the structure covering a building. Plural **roofs** (a common spelling trap — *rooves* is archaic/nonstandard).
- In the passage it appears as the compound **flat roof** (term 18), which is the Modernist signature pitched against the older **pitched roof / gabled roof**.

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **a {flat, pitched, sloping, tiled, thatched, slate, gabled} roof** · **the roof leaks / collapses / terrace** (BrE *roof terrace*)
- Idioms: **hit / go through the roof** (rise sharply; explode in anger) · **raise the roof** (cheer loudly) · **a roof over one's head** (shelter)
- **roof garden / roof tile / rooftop** — note *rooftop* (n/adj) is the flat surface; *roof garden* is the planted one.

---

### 16. PURISM — `/ˈpjʊrɪzəm/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Noun (art/architecture):** a movement and attitude that prizes **purity of form** and rejects decoration. In painting, **Purism** (Le Corbusier and Amédée Ozenfant, after Cubism, c. 1918) argued for clean, ordered, machine-like forms. In architecture it feeds directly into **Modernism** and the **International Style**.
- The general (lower-case) sense: an insistence on keeping a language/style "pure" and free of outside influence.

#### Word family
- **pure** (adj) · **purely** (adv) · **purity** (n) · **purism** (n) · **purist** (n — a person; often mildly critical, as in *a language purist*)
- Related movements worth knowing: **Minimalism**, **Functionalism** (*form follows function*), **Brutalism** (raw exposed concrete; see term 17).

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **an adherence to purism** · **a purist approach** · **architectural / linguistic purism**
- The useful contrast: **purism** removes what is not essential; **ornament** adds what is not structural. Modernism's slogan — **"less is more"** (Mies van der Rohe) — is purism in five syllables.

---

### 17. EXAGGERATION — `/ɪɡˌzædʒəˈreɪʃən/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Noun:** the act of representing something as larger, more important, or more extreme than it is. In design and art, **exaggerated forms** are a deliberate expressive choice (oversized features, distorted proportions) — not an error.
- Note the spelling: **exaggeration** has a double *g* and a single *t* — one of the most commonly misspelled IELTS words.

#### Word family & collocations
- **exaggerate** (v) · **exaggerated** (adj) · **exaggeratedly** (adv)
- **a gross / wild / slight exaggeration** · **without exaggeration** · **it is no exaggeration to say that …** (a strong academic opener)
- Near-synonyms by register: **overstatement** (formal) · **hyperbole** (literary/formal — deliberate, often rhetorical) · **embellishment** (adding detail) · **overdramatisation**

#### Viet-Glish pitfall vs. Band 7.0+ alternative
- ❌ *Viet-Glish mistake:* "The roof is exaggerating big."
- ✅ *Native standard:* "The roof is **exaggeratedly large**" OR "The roof is **oversized to the point of caricature.**"

---

### 18. FLAT ROOF — `/flæt ruːf/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Noun:** a roof with no pitch (or a very slight one, for drainage) — essentially a horizontal surface, often used as a terrace or planted as a roof garden.
- Historically the **flat roof is the emblem of Modernism**: it broke with the pitched roof of the vernacular and classical tradition. Le Corbusier listed the *toit-terrasse* (roof terrace) among his **Five Points of Architecture**; Walter Gropius and the Bauhaus used it at the **Dessau** building (1925–26). Its critics blamed it for leaks and for ignoring local climate (rain, snow).

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **a flat roof {leaks, terraces, garden}** · **install / retrofit a flat roof** · **green / accessible / inverted flat roof**
- Contrast: **pitched roof**, **gable roof**, **hipped roof**, **mansard roof**, **shed roof**. The *choice* of roof type is often the clearest single signal of a building's period and ideology — which is why it is worth a full note.

### 19. PURE — `/pjʊr/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Adjective:** not mixed; free from anything foreign or contaminating. In architecture and design, **pure form** = a form reduced to essentials, with no ornament and no added material.
- Also: **pure mathematics** (theory for its own sake), **pure research** (basic research), **pure play** (business — a company in one line of business).

#### Word family & collocations
- **purely** (adv — *purely aesthetic*) · **purity** (n) · **purify / purification** · **impure**
- **pure {form, white, coincidence, speculation, luck}** · **purely {decorative, functional, coincidental}**
- Note the pairing with term 16: **pure** is the adjective, **purism** the ideology, **purist** the believer.

---

### 20. REVOLUTIONARY — `/ˌrevəˈluːʃəneri/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Adjective:** causing or involving a complete, fundamental change — *a revolutionary design*, *a revolutionary idea*.
- **Noun:** a person who leads/participates in a revolution.
- Register warning: *revolutionary* is a strong claim. In academic writing, **revolutionary** vs **evolutionary** is a real distinction: **radical / disruptive / ground-breaking** vs **incremental / gradual**.

#### Word family & collocations
- **revolutionise / revolutionize** (v) · **revolution** (n) · **revolutionary** (adj/n) · **evolve / evolution / evolutionary** (the antonym frame)
- **a revolutionary {idea, breakthrough, approach, change}** · **revolutionise {an industry, the way we …, transport}**
- Be precise: **innovation** (new and useful) ≠ **invention** (first creation) ≠ **revolution** (wholesale replacement). Modernism in architecture *was* framed as a revolution against ornament.

### 21. CONCRETE — `/ˈkɑːŋkriːt/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Noun (material):** a composite of cement, water, sand and aggregate that sets hard — the dominant modern structural material. Verb/noun **cast** concrete; **reinforced concrete** adds steel bars to carry tension; **prestressed** concrete is tensioned in advance.
- ⚠ **Heteronym alert:** as a **noun/adjective** (the material, or "specific/real") it is `/ˈkɑːŋkriːt/`; as the **adjective meaning "specific, tangible"** (*concrete evidence, concrete steps*) some speakers shift the stress to `/kənˈkriːt/`. The *meaning* distinction is the important one.

| Sense | Example |
|---|---|
| material | *a reinforced concrete frame* |
| specific/real | *concrete evidence* · *take concrete action* |

#### Confusables — concrete vs cement
- **Cement** is the grey powder — the *binder*.
- **Concrete** is cement + water + aggregate — the *finished material*.
- Saying "the building is made of cement" is a classic lexical slip; the building is made of **concrete** (which contains cement).

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **reinforced / prestressed / poured / precast / exposed concrete** · **a concrete {frame, slab, wall, jungle}** · **concrete evidence / proposal / example / steps**
- **Brutalism** (*béton brut* = raw concrete) is the style built on **exposed concrete** (see *expose*, term 14).

### 22. BRICK — `/brɪk/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Noun:** a small rectangular block of fired clay (or concrete) used in walls. The *older* wall material that Modernism's exposed concrete reacted against.
- **Adjective:** *brick* (a brick wall) — note it stays singular in compounds: *a **brick** wall*, *a **brick** house*, not *bricks wall*.

#### Collocations, idioms & pairings
- **a {red, yellow, cladding, facing, engineering} brick** · **lay a brick / bricklayer / bricklaying** · **brick and mortar** (physical as opposed to online: *a brick-and-mortar shop*)
- Idioms: **hit a brick wall** (bị tắc hẳn) · **bang one's head against a brick wall** · **a brick short of a load** (informal — not very clever) · **like a ton of bricks** (very forcefully)
- Pairing with *concrete*: the historical shift **brick → concrete/steel/glass** is the material story of 20th-century architecture.

### 23. ANCIENT — `/ˈeɪnʃənt/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Adjective:** belonging to the very distant past, especially the civilisations of the Mediterranean and the Near East — *ancient Egypt*, *ancient Rome*, *the ancient world*. In the passage it contrasts an *ancient* urban fabric (the souk) with *modern/revolutionary* architecture.
- Scale note: **ancient** (thousands of years) › **medieval** (roughly 5th–15th c.) › **old / historic** › **heritage / listed** (protected) › **contemporary**.

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **ancient {history, ruins, civilisation, monument, Greece/Rome}** · **the ancient world** · **since ancient times**
- ⚠ Avoid the Viet-Glish *"ancient"* for merely "old" — a 100-year-old house is **old / historic**, not ancient.

#### Definition & domain context

- **Verb (follow/trace):** to follow the course or development of — *track progress*, *track a parcel*, *track spending*, *keep track of*. In software, **track** is central: *issue tracking*, *track changes*, *version tracking* (see Section K).

### 25. WONDER — `/ˈwʌndər/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Noun:** a feeling of awe, and the *thing* that causes it — *the wonders of ancient architecture*, *one of the wonders of the world*, **the Seven Wonders**. Also **a wonder** = a surprising fact.

### 26. MONITOR — `/ˈmɑːnɪtər/` · 27. MONITORING — `/ˈmɑːnɪtərɪŋ/`

#### Definition & domain context
- **Verb (the passage's use):** to **observe and measure something over time** — *monitor air quality*, *monitor traffic levels*, *monitor noise*. Crucially, *monitor* implies **continuity** and **measurement**, not a single look. This is why cities *monitor* before they *regulate*: you cannot price a traffic fee fairly without baseline data.
- **Noun:** a display screen; also a person/device that monitors (e.g. a *hall monitor*, an *air-quality monitor*).
- **Monitoring** (n): the ongoing activity — *traffic monitoring*, *environmental monitoring*, *continuous monitoring*.

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **monitor {air quality, traffic, emissions, progress, performance, a situation}** · **closely / continuously / carefully monitor** · **set up / install / maintain a monitoring system**
- **under monitoring / under observation** · **a monitoring station / programme / report**
- The engineering family it belongs to: **observe → measure → monitor → alert → act** (the loop behind observability and MLOps drift monitoring — see Section K).

#### Viet-Glish pitfall vs. Band 7.0+ alternative
- ❌ *Viet-Glish mistake:* "The city monitors the air one time."
- ✅ *Native standard:* "The city **continuously monitors** air quality" (monitoring is a sustained process, so *one time* contradicts the verb).

### 28. MODERATE — `/ˈmɑːdərət/` (adj) · `/ˈmɑːdəreɪt/` (v)

#### Definition & domain context
- **Adjective** (stress on the first syllable): average in amount/intensity — neither extreme — *moderate speed*, *moderate temperature*, *a moderate increase*, *moderate rainfall*.
- **Verb** (stress on the final syllable): to reduce intensity or to chair a discussion — *to moderate a debate*, *the effects were moderated by X*.
- **Noun** (also first-syllable stress): a person with middle-of-the-road views.

⚠ **Heteronym** — the noun/adjective and the verb are spelled the same but stressed differently. This is a high-value IELTS Listening/pronunciation point.

#### Collocations & native pairings
- **moderate {income, growth, climate, risk, success}** · **in moderation** (điều độ) · **moderate views / a moderate stance**
- **moderately {successful, priced, difficult}**
- Scale neighbours: **negligible → slight → moderate → considerable → severe**.

#### Cross-domain hook
In economics, **moderation** and the "Goldilocks" middle are everywhere — central banks aim for **moderate inflation**. And the statistical **moderator variable** (a third variable that changes the strength of a relationship) is the same word doing technical work.

## 🔗 Cross-Domain Bridge (Tech ↔ Business / Economics)

1. **The congestion charge is a Pigouvian tax on a negative externality.**
   Driving imposes costs on others — **exhaust fumes**, noise, congestion, road wear — that the driver does not pay. Economists call this a **negative externality**. Arthur Pigou's fix is to put a price equal to the *marginal external cost* onto the activity, so the driver internalises it:
   $$P_{\text{social}} = P_{\text{private}} + MEC$$
   A traffic fee aims at $MEC$; the observed behaviour (drivers switching to transit, or paying and staying) reveals how close the fee came. This is exactly why the city must first **monitor** (term 26) — you cannot calibrate a Pigouvian price without measuring the externality.
2. **"Concrete" vs. "cement" is a materials-supply-chain lesson.**
   Cement is a **binder** produced by burning limestone and clay (a major $\mathrm{CO_2}$ source — roughly 7–8% of global emissions); concrete is the *composite* with sand, aggregate and water. Confusing the two is not just a word error: the binder, not the aggregate, drives concrete's carbon footprint, and **low-carbon / geopolymer concrete** is an active research and business area — the same "name the material precisely, or you misprice the input" lesson as in a bill of materials.
3. **Purism's "pure form" is the same move as minimising an interface.**
   A purist architect strips away ornament until only structure remains — *expose the concrete, drop the decoration*. A software architect does the same to an API: remove everything not required by the contract, leave the **interface** exposed and the implementation hidden. Both are fights against *accidental complexity*, and both pay for their purity in flexibility (a pure form is hard to extend, a minimal API is hard to change).

## 📝 Research Journey

- **Why I'm researching this:** the words arrived in two lookup batches from one IELTS passage about a city centre and a modern-architecture exhibition. They are the vocabulary of **the built environment** — precisely the domain where urban policy (traffic fees, pedestrians, emissions) meets design (flat roofs, exposed concrete, purism). For an AI Engineer, this is more relevant than it looks: the same city-scale **monitoring → measurement → regulation** loop is the loop behind smart-city data systems and urban ML.
- **What I struggled with:** the **specialist senses hiding inside plain words**. *Construction* looked like "building" until *a social construction* appeared; *expose* looked like "endanger" until *exposed brick* appeared; *capture* looked like "seize" until *capture light* appeared. The fix was to always ask *"which scene is this word painting?"* before choosing a translation.
- **Aha moments:** (1) **Concrete ≠ cement** — one is the material, the other only the binder, and the error hides a real carbon story. (2) **A traffic fee is a Pigouvian tax** — the language of urban policy is the language of externalities, and the fee only works if the city first *monitors*. (3) **Purism and minimal APIs are the same move** — strip to structure, expose the interface, hide the implementation. (4) **A building's roof type is its ideology** — flat roof = Modernism, pitched roof = tradition; a single word carries an entire movement.
- **Career link:** an AI Engineer working on smart cities, mobility, or sustainability has to speak both languages at once — the economics of **congestion pricing / externalities** and the engineering of **monitoring systems**, plus the design vocabulary to read an architecture brief. Naming the material (*concrete*, not *cement*), the policy (*congestion charge*, not *city fee*), and the process (*continuous monitoring*, not *monitor once*) is what separates a clear technical proposal from a translated one.
