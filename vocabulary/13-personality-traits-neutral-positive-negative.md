## 🧠 Detailed Vocabulary Analysis

### 0. The five words that will get you in trouble

Before anything else: the board's three groups are **not** English's three groups. A Vietnamese "chỉ tính cách" board sorts words by *how the word feels in Vietnamese*, but English attaches sentiment differently. Five items in the Neutral column will not stay neutral in English:

| Word | On the board | What a native speaker hears | Use this instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| **sensitive** | neutral / quietly positive (nhạy cảm, tinh tế) | *easily offended, over-reacting* — **negative** | **empathetic**, **perceptive**, **attuned to** |
| **nonchalant** | neutral (bình thản) | *unconcerned* — **mildly negative** | **easygoing**, **unfazed**, **laid-back** |
| **unemotional** | neutral (lý trí, không cảm xúc thừa) | *robotic, cold* — **unnatural for a person** | **composed**, **level-headed**, **unflappable** |
| **passive** | neutral (thụ động) | *doing nothing* — **negative in English** | keep it, but expect it to read as criticism |
| **logical** | neutral (lý trí) | praise *and* *coldness* — the English reader chooses | **analytical** (safe), *rational* (formal) |

And the mirror case: **impersonal** on the board means *không mang tính cá nhân*, which is exactly the **positive professional sense** in English. See §1.17.

> **The general rule:** a word is "positive" in a personality chart because of **the behaviour it names**, and "negative" in English because of **the judgement it implies to a listener**. Vietnamese lets you describe a person without judging; English adjectives carry an implied verdict. That is why translating a personality list word-for-word into a CV produces sentences that sound like accusations — the same failure documented for `stage` and `leverage` in [[vocabulary/12-one-vietnamese-word-many-english-words.md]].

### 1. NEUTRAL — 18 board cells (19 word items), and the trap in each

#### 1.1 introvert / extrovert — the `-vert` family and its spelling

| Form | Adjective | Noun | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| intro- | **introverted** | **introvert** | the personality type |
| extro- | **extroverted** | **extrovert** | `extro-`, not `extra-` |
| ambi- | — | **ambivert** | informal coinage, split down the middle |

- ❌ `introvert` → not *introvertly*. The adverb exists but is rare and academic; say **"she is quite introverted"**.
- ✅ Both **introvert** (n.) and **introverted** (adj.) are correct: *"I'm an introvert"* · *"I'm introverted"*. Same for extrovert/extroverted.
- The `extra-` prefix in English means **additional** (*extra work*), which is exactly why *extrovert* with an `a` looks wrong — and why the related-looking *extrapolate, extract, extracurricular* all mean **"move or take outside"** (compare `vocabulary/05-extraordinary-deep-dive.md`, where *extraordinary* = "outside the normal order").
- **Collocations:** *a natural introvert* · *introverted by nature* · *an extroverted personality* · *socially outgoing and extroverted*.
- 📖 **Origin:** the pair comes from **Jung** (*Psychological Types*, 1921) via **MBTI** (Briggs & Myers). Jung's contribution was not the label but the claim that attention and energy flow in opposite directions.
- ⚠️ **The rebrand:** introversion used to be treated as a defect; since **Susan Cain, *Quiet* (2012)** it is marketable — *"our quiet strengths"*, *introverted leadership*. So in English the *same word* flipped valence within a decade. This is why "neutral" needs a date attached to it.

#### 1.2 curious — the complement trap

`curious` almost never stands alone as a complete description in careful English:

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| *curious **about** + noun* | **curious about** how neural networks actually learn |
| *curious **to know / learn** + clause* | **curious to learn** how it works under the hood |
| *a **curious** + noun* | *a **curious** mind* · *a **curious** child* |
| *it **curious** + object* | *It **curious** me how he knew.* ← most advanced |

Compare the adjectives that *do* stand alone: *analytical*, *patient*, *energetic*. **`curious` always asks a question; it never finishes one.** The noun is **curiosity** (*out of curiosity*, *an insatiable curiosity for X*), and a curious person is not necessarily *inquisitive*, a formal word for the same quality.

#### 1.3 passive — one word, two sciences

| Sense | Field | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **passive** (person) | psychology | receiving rather than initiating; non-assertive |
| **passive voice** | grammar | *"She **was hired**"* — the doer is the object |
| **passive resistance** | physics | friction that opposes motion without storing energy |
| **passive cooling** | engineering | cooling by radiation, no active circulation |

⚠️ **Two phrasal traps:** `passive-aggressive` is a *different* personality label (hostile through indirect obstruction) and is strongly negative; `passive income` is a *positive* finance term. The technical senses are consistent: a passive system is one that does nothing without an input. A passive person in psychology is the same idea applied to behaviour.

#### 1.4 nonchalant — the one word whose board-neutrality does not survive

`nonchalant` is formally "calm and unbothered", and it is a genuine, precise, C1 word. But native usage has drifted: **"nonchalant about his wife's illness" reads as *he did not care*.** It is not in the emotional-neutral zone any more; it sits just inside negative.

| Instead of | Say | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| nonchalant about a problem | **unfazed by** / **relaxed about** | not worried |
| nonchalant in public | **easygoing**, **laid-back** | casual by nature |
| nonchalant in an achievement | **understated**, **unassuming** | success without showing it |

✅ Positive collocation: *a **nonchalant** confidence* — here it is praise, because the *confidence* carries it. The word needs a **positive or neutral object** to stay safe.

#### 1.5 adaptable vs flexible — the single most misused pair in Asian English

| | **adaptable** | **flexible** |
|---|---|---|
| Core idea | can *change* | can *bend* / be modified |
| Goes with people | *a highly **adaptable** engineer* | ❌ *a flexible engineer* (= inconsistent) |
| Goes with schedules/plans | ❌ *adaptable hours* | *fully **flexible** working hours* |
| Goes with rules/plans | *an **adaptable** plan* | *a **flexible** deadline* |
| Job-ad frequency | high | very high |

**Rule of thumb:** *flexible* describes something that **has been allowed to change**; *adaptable* describes a **person who can change**. And ❌ never write *"I am flexible with overtime"* — this reads as *"I will work whatever hours suit you"*, which is not a compliment.

#### 1.6 energetic — why you were right to mark it P

The board put *energetic* in Neutral; in English it is a **compliment about a person** and neutral only about a *thing*:

| Sense | Example | Polarity |
|---|---|---|
| a person | *She's incredibly **energetic** — she runs 10 km before work* | ➡️ **Positive** |
| an activity | *an **energetic** debate between the two labs* | neutral |
| an activity | ***energetic** research* (materials science: high stored energy) | technical, neutral |
| a warning | *⚠️ an **energetic** child who never sits still* | **negative** in context |

`energetic` is also the everyday word that business English upgrades: *we need an **energetic** and **dynamic** salesperson* (see the register ladder in §3.6). The same idea in a CV reads better as **dynamic** or **driven**.

#### 1.7 sensitive — a word with a hidden polarity switch

The load-bearing detail is **what is being sensed** and **who is feeling**:

| Pattern | Example | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| sensitive **to** + stimulus | *very **sensitive to** small changes in temperature* | neutral, positive |
| sensitive **to** + a person's feelings | *too **sensitive to** criticism* | **negative** — the standard collocation is `to` |
| sensitive **about** + a topic | *sensitive about his appearance* | ⚠️ `about` is used, but it is less fixed than `to` — prefer **`sensitive to`** |
| sensitive skin / instrument | *a **sensitive** microphone* | technical |
| sensitive information | *⚠️ we store **sensitive** customer data* | formal, security context |
| sensitive topic | *a politically **sensitive** issue* | formal, "be careful" |

❌ **Never** say *"I'm a sensitive person"* in English to mean *empathetic* — that is exactly how "he can't take a joke" starts. Say **empathetic** (feels with others), **perceptive** (notices detail), **attuned** (*attuned to people's needs*), or **tactful**.

#### 1.8 realistic — the word between optimism and pessimism

*Realistic* is the word that keeps **optimistic** and **pessimistic** apart:

| Word | Claim | Optimism level |
|---|---|---|
| **optimistic** | good things *will* happen | high |
| **realistic** | things *are probably* as they look | matched to evidence |
| **pessimistic** | bad things *will* happen | low |

✅ *a **realistic** assessment* · *a **realistic** price* · *set **realistic** expectations* · *we need to be **realistic** about the timeline* — all praise or neutral. ❌ *a **realistic** person* is rare, because in English realism attaches to **judgements, not people**. Say *down-to-earth* or *practical* for a person. This is also why the board can hold both *realistic* (Neutral) and *optimistic* (Positive) without contradiction.

#### 1.9 risk-taking / risk-taker — polarity is set by the field

| Field | Reading | Example |
|---|---|---|
| entrepreneurship / product | **Positive** | *Startups need **risk-takers**.* |
| engineering | **Positive** | *a calculated, documented **risk** in the release plan* |
| everyday behaviour | **negative** | *he's a bit of a **risk-taker** with money* |
| sport / safety | **negative** | *dangerous **risk-taking*** |

- ✅ **a risk-taker** (the person) / **risk-taking** (the behaviour) / **to take risks** (the verb) / **a calculated risk** (the safe version) / **at risk** (the danger sense). Note the hyphen only in the compound adjective.
- ★ **Ladder for the safe version:** *bold* → *ambitious* → *a calculated risk* → *reckless* (**negative**) → *careless* (**worse**). The whole difference between *ambitious* and *reckless* is whether the risk was *assessed*.

#### 1.10 pragmatic — the most useful word in the Neutral column

`pragmatic` = **judging by what actually works rather than by theory or principle.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| a **pragmatic** approach / solution / decision | *We took a **pragmatic** approach: ship it, then measure.* |
| **pragmatically** (adverb) | *Let's solve it **pragmatically** and not argue about principles.* |
| noun: **pragmatism** | *He was known for his **pragmatism**.* |

⚠️ Careful: **pragmatic** is not the opposite of *idealistic* in a moral sense — a *pragmatic solution* is not a *cheap* one. ⚠️ And **pragmatic** in American English can also mean *unrealistically about things*: *"we need to be pragmatic about deadlines"* can mean *"let's lower our standards"*, which is the opposite of the intended praise. ✅ Use the explicit form: **practical, results-driven, pragmatic about scope**.

#### 1.11 independent vs self-reliant vs solo

Three words the board mixes (one in Neutral, one in Column 1) plus a word English adds:

| Word | Focus | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **independent** | not controlled by others | *an independent researcher · independent of the sponsor* |
| **self-reliant** | not needing help to do it yourself | *a self-reliant learner* |
| **solo** | done alone, by choice | *solo project · solo contributor · solo mode* |

✅ *independent **of** X* (never *from*, in this sense) · *financially **independent*** · *be **independent** of your parents' money* · *work **independently***.
⚠️ In British English, **"independent" also means not belonging to a political party** — a newspaper might report *"an independent MP"*. Useful for reading, useless for describing yourself.

#### 1.12 intuitive — praise in product, warning in psychology

This word changes polarity **by field**, and this is one of the most useful things in the note:

| Field | Sense | Polarity |
|---|---|---|
| product / UX | easy to grasp without instructions | **Positive** — *an **intuitive** interface* |
| hiring / CV | quick learner, grasps concepts | **Positive** |
| decision-making psychology | reasoning you cannot justify — System 1 | **cautionary** — *trusting intuition is risky* |
| skill description | practised, pattern-based skill (a nurse's intuition) | **Positive** |

✅ *an **intuitive** grasp of the problem* · *something **intuitive** to explain* · *intuitive reasoning* (the technical term).
⚠️ *"My answer is just an **intuitive** feeling"* is a red flag in a technical interview. The technical term for the good version is **heuristic**, and the honest framing is *"based on experience with X"*. This is Kahneman's **System 1 vs System 2** in one word: intuition is fast, cheap, and confident — which is why it is unreliable and why the field uses the same word for both a gift and a bias. It is also the vocabulary asymmetry with **impulsive**: a Vietnamese speaker often treats *trực giác* and *bốc đồng* as two faces of the same honesty, while English splits them into one compliment and one criticism.

#### 1.13 logical vs analytical vs rational — the coldness gradient

| Word | Register | Also reads as |
|---|---|---|
| **logical** | neutral → mild praise | *cold, emotionless* (English distrusts pure logic in people) |
| **analytical** | **the safe praise** | the standard adjective for engineers and researchers |
| **rational** | formal, business | *a rational decision* ✅ · *"he's too rational"* ❌ |

✅ *an **analytical** mind* · *strong **analytical** and problem-solving skills* (this exact phrase is in most data-science job descriptions) · *break something down **analytically***. The noun is **analysis** → **analyst** → **analyse/analyze** (BrE/AmE spelling).

#### 1.14 unemotional — the one word to avoid describing a person

**`unemotional` about a person is not a compliment in English.** It suggests a machine. What the board calls *không cảm xúc thừa* — calm under pressure — is:

| Goal | Correct word |
|---|---|
| doesn't panic | **composed**, **level-headed**, **unflappable** |
| steady in an emergency | **calm**, **cool-headed** |
| reacts on facts, not feelings | **rational**, **objective** |
| psychology term for low reactivity | **emotionally stable** / **low reactivity** (Eysenck: low arousal) |

❌ *"My weakness is that I'm **unemotional**"* in a CV — reads as *I don't care*.
✅ *"I stay **composed** under pressure."*
The word itself is alive, but in **product design** (*an unemotional design language — blue, grey, sans-serif*) and **finance** (*unemotional investing*) — which is exactly the register the board's Neutral column is trying to reach.

#### 1.15 direct — two pronunciations, two shades

| Part of speech | Pronunciation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **adjective** (straightforward) | `/dəˈrekt/` | *a **direct** answer* · *we need **direct** communication* |
| **verb** (to aim at, to guide) | `/dɪˈrekt/` | *she **directed** the team* |

✅ *a **direct** person* · *being **direct** with you* · *in **direct** contrast* · *contact details* (**direct**, not *directly*).
⚠️ The family: **directly** (adverb) · **direction** · **director** · **directive** (a written instruction, slightly formal/negative). The neighbouring shades are covered in §3 and §4.4 — *direct* is the safest word in the family, *blunt* the most dangerous.

#### 1.16 analytical — the engineer's word, and how to use it in a CV

*Analytical* is the highest-frequency personality adjective in technical job ads. It has a specific technique meaning, and the noun pair is **analysis / analyst / analyse**.

✅ Collocations that actually appear in job ads: *strong analytical skills* · *analytical thinking* · *an analytical approach to problem-solving* · *analytical mindset* · *data-driven and analytical*.
⚠️ **`analysis` vs `analytic`** — the adjectives: **analytical** (of a person or a process) and **analytic** (mathematical; rarer). In statistics, **analytic** appears in *analytic function* and *analytic continuation*.
★ **Upgrade path for a CV:** *analytical* → *data-driven* → *insight-oriented* → *you turned a 12% latency regression into a 3% one*.

#### 1.17 impersonal — the sleeper of the Neutral column ⭐

**`impersonal` is a high-value professional English word that the board put in Neutral for the wrong reason.** Two senses, opposite in effect:

| Sense | Example | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| **not about a specific person** (professional) | *an **impersonal** process* · *an **impersonal** tone* · ***impersonal** communication* | ✅ **Positive: objective, fair, human** |
| **cold, without human warmth** (human) | *an **impersonal** manner* · *the building was **impersonal*** | ❌ Negative: unfeeling |

*Impersonal communication* is a **core business-English term** — communication that puts the *message* first and the *relationship* second. E-mail, notices, invoices, knowledge-base articles: all impersonal communication. Pair it with *impersonal communication style* / *written in an impersonal register*.

⚠️ In a self-description, do not use the second sense. ✅ *"I aim for an **impersonal** code review — the code is judged, not the author."* (Better still: *"**blameless** post-mortems"* — the Agile version of the same idea.)

#### 1.18 quiet — the rebrand, in three words

*Quiet* is neutral in a personality chart and *positive* in modern professional English. The vocabulary of "quiet confidence" is now a **brand**: *quiet confidence*, *quiet authority*, *quiet leadership*.

✅ *a **quiet** but firm presence* · ***quiet** confidence* · *the **quiet** confidence of someone who knows they are right* · *a **quiet** place to work*.
⚠️ Ambiguity: *quiet* also means *low-volume* (*a quiet voice*) and *not busy* (*a quiet street*).

### 2. POSITIVE COLUMN 1 — 17 words of character

> **Why this column feels different.** These 17 words describe **character** — how you treat people, how you keep your word, how much you give of yourself. The two right-hand columns describe **temperament and social style** — how you behave socially and how much energy you carry. In English the same distinction exists: *integrity* words (sincere, loyal, honest, trustworthy) vs *style* words (outgoing, dynamic, cheerful). ⚠️ **They are not interchangeable.** A person can be a *dynamic, outgoing, sociable* team member and still be **arrogant** and **dishonest**. That is the single most important insight in this note: **character words (Column 1) are the ones that carry moral weight in English; social-style words (Columns 2 & 3) are descriptive.**

#### 2.1 The -ness → adjective engine ⭐

Every `-ness` noun on the board has a matching adjective, but the **suffix is not random** — it follows six patterns. This is the highest-yield thing on the whiteboard, because it replaces a memorised list with six rules you can apply to any new `-ness` noun you meet.

| # | Rule | Example | More |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | drop `-ness` | boldness → **bold** | calmness → **calm** · darkness → **dark** |
| **B** | `-ness` → `-ful` | faithfulness → **faithful** · thoughtfulness → **thoughtful** | carelessness → **careless** · hopelessness → **hopeless** |
| **C** | `-ness` → `-ing` | forgiveness → **forgiving** · willingness → **willing** | ⚠️ not *forgiveful* (never a word) |
| **D** | `-ness` → `-ive` / `-ous` / `-ent` | decisiveness → **decisive** · confidence → **confident** · curiosity → **curious** · generosity → **generous** | ⚠️ not *decisent*, *curiety* |
| **E** | `-ness` → `-y` | happiness → **happy** · laziness → **lazy** | readiness → **ready** |
| **F** | `-ty` → `-st` / `-al` / `-ous` / `-e` / `-r` | modesty → **modest** · honesty → **honest** · anxiety → **anxious** · sincerity → **sincere** · loyalty → **loyal** · clarity → **clear** | ⚠️ **`-ty` → `-r` is irregular**: *purity* → pure, *novelty* → novel |

**Applied to the board's five nouns:**

| Noun on the board | Adjective | Rule | ❌ The common wrong form |
|-------------------|-----------|------|--------------------------|
| **Gentleness** | **gentle** | ⚠️ **no rule needed** — see below | *gentlent* · *gentleful* |
| **Faithfulness** | **faithful** | **B** | ⚠️ *faithless* (= the opposite!) |
| **Forgiveness** | **forgiving** | **C** | *forgiveful* · *forgivingness* |
| **Boldness** | **bold** | **A** | — |
| **Decisiveness** | **decisive** | **D** | *decisionive* |

⚠️ **Why *gentleness* is the odd one out — and why that is instructive.** These rules run **from the noun to the adjective**, and nearly every `-ness` noun was itself *made from* the adjective (*bold* → *boldness*, *faithful* → *faithfulness*). What you are learning is therefore a **word family**, not a derivation you can run backwards. *Gentle* is the base form and *gentleness* merely keeps its `-le` and adds `-ness*, so no suffix rule is needed at all. The practical consequence: **check the adjective for a silent `-e` before applying any rule** — *gentle, simple, humble* all end in one, which is exactly why learners reach for `-ful` and write ❌ *gentleful*.

⚠️ **The three errors learners actually make:**
1. **Adding `-ness` to a verb:** ❌ *forgivness* · *boldnessful* → ✅ *forgiveness* · *boldness*
2. **Choosing `-ful` when the suffix is `-ing`:** ❌ *forgiveful* → ✅ **forgiving**
3. **Inventing a suffix that does not exist:** ❌ *decisionive* · *carefullness* → ✅ *decisiveness* · *carefulness*

**And the reason the two forms behave differently at all:** the **adjective is a trait** (a *stable* tendency, inferred over time, a thing you are) and the **`-ness` noun is a state** (a *measurable quantity* at a moment, a thing you have). This is the trait/state distinction in personality psychology, and it is why research reports *scored high on Neuroticism (N)* while people *describe themselves as nervous*. The `-ness` noun is also the one that can be **counted** — a happiness index, a kindness metric, a risk score. Professional English leans on the `-ness` nouns when it wants to sound measurable and clinical. (See §6.2.)

#### 2.2 The abstract-virtue cluster — where the precision lives

| Word | What it actually credits | Strongest collocation | Common weaker word |
|---|---|---|---|
| **sincere** | *genuine feeling, no hidden agenda* | *a **sincere** apology / **sincere** thanks* | honest (broader: also about not lying) |
| **honest** | *not lying, not cheating* | *an **honest** assessment* | truthful (about one specific statement) |
| **genuine** | *real, not a copy — also used of objects* | *a **genuine** concern* | authentic (more about culture/art) |
| **candid** | *voluntarily saying the awkward truth* | *a **candid** answer* | frank (sometimes negative) |
| **forthright** | *blunt but well-meant* | *a **forthright** discussion* | ⚠️ often *slightly negative* |

✅ *a **sincere** effort* · *she was **sincere** about wanting to help* · *I'm **sincere** in my apologies*.
⚠️ *sincere* does not mean *intense*. ❌ *"a **sincere** scream"* — use **a blood-curdling / piercing scream**.

#### 2.3 tactful ↔ diplomatic — and a false friend in the word family

| Word | Focus | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **tactful** | care for the **feelings** of one person | *a **tactful** way to raise the issue* · *a **tactful** refusal* |
| **diplomatic** | care for the **situation**, avoiding conflict | *a **diplomatic** solution* · *diplomatic relations* |
| **prudent** | careful to avoid loss, money | *a **prudent** decision* · *a **prudent** reserve* |
| **judicious** | showing good judgement in *when/how* to act | *a **judicious** use of praise* |

⚠️ **Spelling & stress:** **courteous** `/ˈkɜː.ti.əs/` — silent `e`, stress on the first syllable. ❌ *courteuos* · *courgeous* · stressing the second syllable.
⚠️ **False friend inside the family:** **tactful** ← Latin *tangere* "to touch" (the same root as **tact** = the sense of touch, and **tangent**, **tactile**), but **tactic / tactics** ← Greek *taxis* "arrangement" and are **not** related. Do not build a false story from the similarity.
✅ **Collocations:** *a **courteous** refusal* · *it would be **courteous** to reply* · *extremely **courteous** to everyone* · *a **courteous** and professional manner*.
⚠️ **Courtesy is a noun, not a verb.** ❌ *"I will **courtesy** you"* → ✅ *"I will **extend** you the **courtesy** of a reply."*

#### 2.4 dependable vs reliable vs trustworthy

| Word | Goes with | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **dependable** | **people, promises, systems** | "you can count on them" |
| **reliable** | **things, machines, data, people** | "consistently performs" |
| **trustworthy** | **people** | "morally honest and reliable" |
| **steady** | people, prices, hands | "does not waver" |

✅ *a **dependable** friend* · *a **dependable** workhorse* · *a **reliable** source* · *a **reliable** car* · *a **trustworthy** colleague*.
⚠️ **The machine/person asymmetry is real:** native usage prefers *a **reliable** car* and *a **dependable** friend*. A CV written by a non-native speaker or by an AI often gets this backwards — and it is the board itself that splits them, putting **dependable** in Column 1 and **reliable** in Columns 2 & 3.

#### 2.5 thoughtful — two senses that are both useful

| Sense | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **considerate** | thinking about other people's feelings | *It was **thoughtful** of you to check on me.* |
| **deliberate, reflective** | thinking carefully; also *thorough* | *a **thoughtful** approach* · *a **thoughtful** reply* |

✅ *a **thoughtful** and well-argued answer* · *a **thoughtful** design* — both mean **good, considered, thorough**.
⚠️ Do not use it for "thorough" in a *process* sense. ❌ *a **thoughtful** integration* → ✅ *a **careful** / **thorough** integration*. ⚠️ Also **thoughtless** = not considerate. ✅ *a **thoughtless** remark*.

#### 2.6 affectionate ⭐ — and the ML field named after it

✅ *an **affectionate** hug* · *she is very **affectionate** with her students* · *an **affectionate** farewell* · *to be **affectionate towards** someone* (⚠️ *not ~~affectionate to~~*).
❌ **Not an intensifier:** *an affectionate punch* means a punch you love.
💡 **Affective Computing** is Rosalind Picard's field (MIT, *Affective Computing*, 1997): computing systems that **recognise and respond to human emotion** from speech, face, gesture, and physiology. Same root, different sense: *affective* = "relating to emotion", not "loving". **This is the family you want to know for an AI career** — see §6.3.

#### 2.7 diligent vs conscientious — the effort/duty split

| Word | Credits | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **diligent** | **effort** | *Did they work hard?* → *a **diligent** student* |
| **conscientious** | **duty** | *Did they do the right thing?* → *a **conscientious** team member* |
| **hard-working** | long hours | *She is **hard-working**.* |

✅ *a **diligent** worker* · *diligent study* · *diligent about records*.
⚠️ Latin *diligere* = "to value highly" — the root sense is **taking care of**, which is why *diligence* once meant **attention to business affairs** (in law: *diligence* = due care). Same family: **diligent**, **diligence**, **indigent** (poor — not related!).
💡 **Conscientiousness** is also the name of one of the **Big Five** domains — see §6.1. The board's *diligent* and the research domain *conscientiousness* are neighbours, not synonyms.

#### 2.8 compassionate vs empathetic vs sympathetic — a real three-way

| Word | Structure | Feeling |
|---|---|---|
| **sympathetic** | **sym-** (together) | I feel *with* you — sorrow for your misfortune |
| **empathetic** | **em-** (in) | I feel *what you feel* — I understand from inside |
| **compassionate** | **com-** + **passion** (suffering) | I feel for you *and want to act* |

✅ *a **compassionate** leader* · *feel **compassion for** the victims* · *a **compassionate** response to the outage* · *a **sympathetic** ear* (idiom).
⚠️ *compassionate* is followed by **for** + compassion, or takes a direct object: *a **compassionate** decision*. ❌ *compassionate to someone* → ✅ *compassionate **towards** someone*.

#### 2.9 humorous — and the spelling trap

✅ *a **humorous** speaker* · *a **humorous** novel* · *a **humorous** person* (a **funny** person sounds like you mean the joke) · *a **self-deprecating** sense of humour* · *use humour to defuse tension*.
❌ **humourous** — the `u` is always dropped: British *humour*, American *humor*, adjective **humorous** everywhere.
⚠️ *humorous* = the ability/quality; *humourless* = a dreary place; the two contrast like *serious*/*funny*, not like *kind*/*cruel*.

#### 2.10 bold, boldness — the typography twin

✅ *a **bold** move* · *too **bold** for a first release* · *be **bold** but be right* · *a **bold** claim* · ***boldness** and vision* · *tackle it with **boldness*** · *in **bold** (typography)*.
⚠️ **Typography sense:** the *Weight* setting in Word, `<b>bold</b>`, "make the heading **bold**". A surprising number of English words have a technical twin (`direct` = direct call, `greedy` = greedy algorithm, `lazy` = lazy loading, `hardcode` = hard-coded).
⚠️ Bold is not brave-with-others: ***a **bold** leader*** (takes risks) ≠ ***a **bold** claim*** (unverified). For courage, use **brave** or **courageous** (Columns 2 & 3).

#### 2.11 The two idioms — and the register warning

**🦫 eager beaver** (informal, mainly AmE)

| Sense | Example | Who says it |
|---|---|---|
| **positive**: a hard worker, an enthusiast | *She's an **eager beaver** — always first in.* | friendly colleagues |
| **negative**: over-eager, exhausting to manage | *an **eager beaver** who jumps into production before reading the docs* | ⚠️ mildly critical |

✅ *an **eager beaver** on the team* · *an **eager** learner* (safe, and the phrase the board's meaning really wants). ⚠️ **Not in a formal interview or a CV.** ✅ **CV-safe:** *"A self-starter who consistently finishes tasks early."*
**Word family:** *eager* (+ eagerness) · *avid* (formal: *an **avid** reader*) · *keen* (BrE: *keen to learn*) · *zealous* (⚠️ can be negative: *zealous* ≈ over-the-top). ★ **Register ladder:** *eager* → *keen* → *avid* → *zealous* (turns negative) → *fanatical*.

**🎯 go-getter** (informal, AmE)

✅ *a real **go-getter*** · *I'm a **go-getter** — I don't wait to be assigned work.*
⚠️ **Informal, and can be read as "pushy"**: *"He's a **go-getter**, but he steamrolls people."*
✅ **CV/interview-safe:** *"I identify opportunities rather than waiting to be assigned them."* · *"Proactive."* · *"Self-starter."*
⚠️ **Not a 3-syllable word:** go-getter (3 syll) `/ˈɡəʊˌɡet.ər/` — not "go-get-ter" as in *golf*.

#### 2.12 gentle, gentleness — the only board word with a *physical* sense

| Sense | Example | Register |
|---|---|---|
| mild in manner or force | *a **gentle** reminder* · *a **gentle** breeze* · *a **gentle** slope* | everyday |
| careful with a delicate person | *be **gentle** with her* | everyday |
| **a gentle slope** / gentle gradient | *a **gentle** increase in resolution* | technical — an RLS policy on a database, a gentle learning rate |
| **a gentle person** | *his **gentle** manner* | everyday, slightly old-fashioned |

✅ *a **gentle** reminder* (**soft** is the standard word: *just a **soft** reminder*) · *a **gentle** introduction to the topic* · *it **gently** rises from 0.2 to 1.0*.
⚠️ ❌ *"You are **gentleness**"* → ✅ *"You are **gentle**"*. In the literal reading, *gentleness* is an uncountable noun: *with **gentleness***.

#### 2.13 faithful, faithfulness — five senses, one word

| Sense | Example | Related |
|---|---|---|
| loyal to a person | *a **faithful** friend* · *a **faithful** dog* | loyal (stronger, more active) |
| loyal to a belief or partner | *a **faithful** follower* | devoted (active) |
| **a true copy** | *a **faithful** reproduction of the original* | accurate (technical) |
| **believing** (the noun: *the faithful*) | *a **faithful** congregation* | church sense |
| **a loyal member of a group** | *I'm a **faithful** listener to your podcast* | loyal |

✅ *remain **faithful** to the original* · *a **faithful** interpretation* · ***with **faithfulness***.
⚠️ ❌ *faithful **to** a promise* → ✅ *faithful **to** his word / keep **faithful** to the spec*.
⚠️ **Opposite trap:** **unfaithful** = having an affair ⚠️. If you mean "the spec drifted", say **inaccurate** or **divergent** — using *unfaithful to the spec* is understood but avoided in sensitive registers.
**Compare:** *devoted* = active, warm · *loyal* = steady, committed · *staunch* = strongly and loudly committed (often political) · *faithful* = steady and traditional.

#### 2.14 forgiving, forgiveness — the `-ing` rule in the wild

✅ *a **forgiving** person* · *a **forgiving** attitude toward mistakes* · *she was **forgiving** about it* · ***with **forgiveness*** · *to **forgive** someone for something*.
⚠️ ❌ ***forgiveful*** — the single most-misspelled item on the whole board. **The rule:** `forgiveness` → **`forgiving`**, never `-ful`.
✅ *It took a lot of **forgiveness** to let that go.*
Note: **forgiving** is not *lenient* (mercy) and not *tolerant* (accepting a difference); it is specifically about **not holding a grudge**.

#### 2.15 self-reliant — the good twin of selfish

| Word | Direction |
|---|---|
| **self-reliant** | "I do it myself" ✅ |
| **selfish** | "I don't care about you" ❌ |
| **selfless** | "I put you first" ✅ |
| **self-confident** | "I trust my own ability" ✅ |
| **self-conscious** | "I'm uncomfortable being observed" ⚠️ |

✅ *a **self-reliant** learner* · *highly **self-reliant** in problem-solving* · *a **self-reliant** team* · *studied in a **self-reliant** way*.
⚠️ **Not the same as *independent***: you can be *self-reliant* and still need people. And ❌ *self-dependent* is a contradiction — the word exists, but only in dependency injection.
💡 **The CV connection:** *"A **self-starter**"* is the job-ad equivalent of *self-reliant* — it says you begin work without instruction. The whole point of a CV adjective is to **pre-empt a negative label**, and *self-reliant* pre-empts **lazy**. See §6.2.

#### 2.16 decisive, decisiveness — the last of the five nouns

| Word | Contrast |
|---|---|
| **decisive** | made a decision; a leader who decides ✅ |
| **decisive** (in a match) | a 3–0 win, settled the matter ✅ |
| **indecisive** | cannot choose ❌ |
| **decisive action** | fast, no hesitation ✅ |
| **a decisive factor** | the single thing that determined the outcome ✅ |
| **a decisive moment** | the turning point ✅ |

✅ *a **decisive** leader* · *took **decisive** action* · *the **decisive** factor in the deployment* · *play **decisively*** · ***with **decisiveness***.
⚠️ **Decisive ≠ rude.** *Decisive* means *fast and clear about what to do*, not *insensitive*. ⚠️ In a *negative* group of personality words, a decisive person is still a *kind* person — the words are independent dimensions, which is the next section.

#### 3.1 The -ing adjectives: caring, and the participle trap

**Caring** is an **-ing adjective** describing a quality. These are extremely common in spoken English and are not the same thing as a present participle (a verb in progress):

| Sentence | Function | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| *He's **caring**.* | **adjective** — quality | anh ấy có tấm lòng / quan tâm người khác |
| *She's **caring** for the plants.* | **verb** — progressive | cô ấy đang chăm sóc cây cối |
| *The result was **surprising**.* | **adjective** — quality | kết quả đáng ngạc nhiên |
| *The baby is **sleeping**.* | **verb** — progressive | em bé đang ngủ |

**Other -ing adjectives in this family:** *caring · outgoing · willing · forgiving · interesting · charming · promising · welcoming · understanding*.
✅ *a **caring** father* · *a **caring** attitude* · ***very **caring** towards her students*** · *caring about the environment*.
⚠️ In business English, *caring* is close to **considerate** or **responsive**; ⚠️ in product design, *user-caring* is unusual — use **user-centred** (a core design term).

#### 3.2 wise — four senses, and one false friend

| Sense | Example |
|---|---|
| good judgement from experience | *a **wise** decision* · *a **wise** mentor* |
| (plural, literary) the old | ***the wise** — "the wisdom of the **wise**"* |
| *(as an interjection)* | *"Well, **wise** of you."* ← sarcastic |
| ➡️ **`anyway`, `likewise`, `otherwise`, `nonetheless`** | **a different `-wise` family** — an adverbial `-wise`, unrelated |

⚠️ **False friend:** the `-wise` in **anyway / likewise / otherwise** has nothing to do with **wise**. In "anyway" and "otherwise" it is an old *in any case / in another manner* sense. Do not connect them in your memory.
✅ *a **wise** choice* · ***no-one is born **wise***** · *it would be **wise** to test first* · *a **wise** use of resources*.
⚠️ ↔ **foolish / unwise / naive / reckless / judicious / prudent / shrewd / astute / sagacious**. ✅ **IELTS upgrade:** *wise* (C1, safe) → *judicious* (formal) → *sagacious* (literary, "wise beyond his years") → *astute* (shrewd, often about people) → *shrewd* (calculating, can be negative).

#### 3.3 responsible — two senses, one word (and a serious CV error)

| Sense | Structure | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **duty / in charge of** | *I'm **responsible for** the API* | có trách nhiệm về / phụ trách |
| **duty / accountable to** | *she's **responsible to** the board* | chịu trách nhiệm trước |
| **reliable, conscientious** | *a **responsible** engineer* | đáng tin, có trách nhiệm với công việc |

✅ *a **responsible** role* · *a **responsible** team member* · *hold someone **responsible** for* · *I'm **responsible for** onboarding* · ***hold yourself responsible***.
⚠️ **The classic beginner error:** using *I'm **responsible** for X* where you mean *"I have authority over X"*. That is *I'm **in charge of** X* or *"X reports to me."* **Being responsible for something does not mean you own it** — the distinction is `in charge of` (authority) vs `responsible for` (accountability), and the board's own Positive Column 1 puts both *dependable* and *decisive* next to this trap.
⚠️ **responsibly** (adverb) is what CVs actually need: *"I delivered the migration **responsibly**, with rollback documented."*

#### 3.4 brave, bold, courageous — the courage family

| Word | Sense | Collocation |
|---|---|---|
| **brave** | not afraid | *a **brave** decision* · *be **brave** enough to ask* · *brave new world* |
| **courageous** | formal, admirable | *a **courageous** stand* · *her **courageous** decision to speak up* |
| **valiant / gallant** | literary, heroic | ⚠️ too grand for normal conversation |
| **bravery / courage** | nouns | ***it takes **courage** to admit you were wrong*** |
| **guts** | informal | *You've **got guts**.* |
| **nerve** | daring, sometimes negative | *the **nerve** to quit mid-sprint* |

⚠️ *brave* + abstract noun (*brave new world*) works, but ❌ *a **brave** website* doesn't. ⚠️ *"too **brave**"* can mean reckless.

#### 3.5 humble vs modest — and a CV lesson

| | **humble** | **modest** |
|---|---|---|
| Core sense | not arrogant; not self-important | not boasting; also **small** in amount |
| About oneself | *stay **humble** even when you win* | *he was **modest** about his success* |
| About money/size | ❌ | *a **modest** salary* · *a **modest** increase* |
| Noun | humility / **humbleness** | modesty |
| Noun trap | ❌ *"a **humbleness** about it"* → ✅ *"with **humility**"* | ✅ *"with **modest** means"* |

✅ *a **humble** approach to learning* · *a **humble** and teachable mindset* · *stay **humble*** · *a **modest** contribution* · ***with **modest** means*.
💡 **`humblebrag`** (noun/verb) is the modern distortion: *"I'm so grateful to be fired"* — a boast disguised as modesty. Mentioning it shows you know the register.
★ **CV translation:** "I am a good listener" → *"I'm **humble** about what I don't know"* / *"I let the **modest** version speak first."* Sells the same trait without the CV cliché.

#### 3.6 optimistic, cheerful, happy, dynamic — the four energies

| Word | Emotion | Field | Collocation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **happy** | general contentment, moderate | everyday | *a **happy** customer* · *I'm **happy with** it* · *I'm **happy to** help* |
| **cheerful** | outwardly bright, a manner | everyday, people & places | *a **cheerful** greeting* · *a **cheerful** room* |
| **optimistic** | expecting good outcomes | business, planning | ***optimistic about** the forecast* · *a **cautiously optimistic** outlook* |
| **dynamic** | always moving, growth-driven | business, CV | *a **dynamic** team* · *a **dynamic** industry* · ***dynamic** growth* |

⚠️ **Three traps:**
1. **happy to ≠ happy about:** *"I'm **happy to** work Saturday"* = sẵn lòng. **"I'm happy with the layout"** = hài lòng với. ❌ *"I am **happy for** your success"* → ✅ *"I'm **glad for you**"* or *"I'm **pleased that** you got it."*
2. **cheerful ≠ happy:** *cheerful* is a **manner** you display; *happy* is a **state** you feel. *a **cheerful** waitress* ✅ (manner) · *a **cheerful** room* ✅ (atmosphere) · *a **cheerful** salary* ❌.
3. **dynamic ≠ a moving object:** *dynamic* for people, companies and organisations, not for a box. ❌ *"this is a dynamic website"* → ✅ *an **interactive** site* (a **dynamic** page is one whose content changes).
⚠️ **optimistic** always needs **about / on**: ***optimistic about** revenue* · ***optimistic on** margins* · *"I'm optimistic"* (bare) is fine but thin.
⚠️ **Optimistic ≠ hopeful:** *hopeful* is emotional, *optimistic* is a forecast. And ≠ *over-optimistic*, which is a criticism.
⚠️ **Spelling families:** *optimist* (n) · *optimistic* (adj) · *optimism* (n) · *optimistically* (adv) · *pessimism* · *pessimistic* · *pessimist*.

#### 3.7 sociable — the single most-missed word on the board

❌ **"She's very social"** = "cô ấy rất hay đăng mạng xã hội" — it describes *social media* use, not personality. ✅ **"She's very sociable"** = cô ấy thân thiện, thích giao lưu.

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **sociable** | enjoys company; a **person** quality |
| **social** | relating to society or social media |
| **socialise / socialize** | to mix with people |
| **sociology** | the study of society |

✅ *a **sociable** person* · *a **sociable** and approachable manner* · ***sociable** and outgoing* · *a **sociable** atmosphere* (of a place) · *How **sociable** are you?* (an IELTS topic).
⚠️ Note the cognate family in computing and networking: **social graph**, **social engineering** (a security term — manipulating people, not networking), **social network**. A **socially engineered** attack is a *human* attack. (See `dev_foundation/linux/01-file-system-paths-and-permissions.md` for the permissions side of who gets access to what.)

#### 3.8 enthusiastic — one verb, three nouns, one preposition

| Form | Sentence |
|---|---|
| **enthusiastic about** + noun/gerund | *She's **enthusiastic about** open source.* ✅ |
| **enthusiastic for** + noun | *They're **enthusiastic for** the new design.* ✅ (BrE) |
| **enthusiasm** | *the team's **enthusiasm** for the project* ✅ |
| **enthusiast** (n) | *a jazz **enthusiast*** / *an **enthusiast** of Korean food* ✅ |
| **enthusiastic** (adj) | *an **enthusiastic** audience* |

⚠️ **BrE vs AmE:** *enthusiast **for** X* (BrE) vs *enthusiast **about** X* (AmE). When unsure, **enthusiastic about** is safe.
⚠️ *enthusiastic* + noun is standard: ***enthusiastic** welcome / **enthusiastic** applause / **enthusiastic** support.*
⚠️ **The ladder:** *enthusiastic about* (strong) → *keen on* (BrE, "I really like it") → *passionate about* (a lasting feeling) → *an **enthusiast** (a person)* → *lukewarm about* (the safe opposite) → *apathetic* (indifferent).

#### 3.9 generous vs stingy — and the two meanings of "cheap"

| Word | Reading |
|---|---|
| **generous** | giving more than needed ✅ |
| **stingy / mean / tight** | giving less than needed ❌ |
| **charitable** | giving, and especially giving to charity ✅ |
| **open-handed / liberal** | giving freely (esp. with money) ✅ |
| **lavish / extravagant** | giving excessively ⚠️ (can be negative) |
| **cheap ❗(1)** | low in price ✅ *"a cheap phone"* |
| **cheap ❗(2)** | stingy, mean ❌ *"he's cheap"* |

⚠️ **"He's cheap"** is nearly always **mean** ❌, not **low-priced** ✅ — the two senses of *cheap* are opposites in effect, and Vietnamese *rẻ* maps only to the first. Compare *a **cheap** trick* and *a **cheap** shot* (rude behaviour) → ❌.
✅ *a **generous** offer* · *too **generous** with the praise* · ***It's very **generous** of you to say that.*** · *a **generous** portion* (BrE: *large*) · *a **generous** donation*.
⚠️ **Preposition:** *generous **with** X* (with money, time, praise) · ***It is **generous of** you to*** + infinitive. ❌ *generous to* + noun.
⚠️ **"Too generous" is often a criticism**: *you're too **generous** with the estimate* = you padded the numbers.

#### 3.10 hospitable — rare in speech, useful in writing

| Sense | Example |
|---|---|
| welcoming to guests | *a **hospitable** host* · *a **hospitable** people* ✅ |
| **a hospitable climate** | *a **hospitable** climate for startups* · *an **inhospitable** environment* ✅ |
| *(informal)* open, easy to talk to | *a **hospitable** atmosphere* ✅ |

✅ *a **hospitable** and welcoming host* · ***hospitable towards** guests* · *a **hospitable** environment for growth*.
⚠️ **Frequency trap:** this is a *written* / formal-review word. In speech, natives say **welcoming**. ⚠️ **inhospitable** is double-barrelled (*in-hospitable*) and more common than *hospitable* in technical writing (*"an inhospitable build environment"*).

#### 3.11 outgoing — the four senses of one word

| Sense | Example | Ambiguity risk |
|---|---|---|
| **socially confident and energetic** | *an **outgoing** person* | ✅ **the personality sense** |
| **leaving a place** | *an **outgoing** flight* | ✅ a different meaning entirely |
| **going out socially** | *I'm **outgoing** tonight* | ✅ but sounds like a plan, not a personality |
| **being sent** | *an **outgoing** message* | ❌ unusual; say *outbound* |

✅ *an **outgoing** and confident individual* · *she's more **outgoing** than me* · *an **outgoing** call* (= a call being dialled).
⚠️ **Don't mix up four different words:** *outgoing* = a social manner · *extroverted* = an energy source · *outspoken* = says what you think · *outward* = external-facing.

#### 3.12 reliable — the word that scales from a person to a message queue

| Context | Collocation |
|---|---|
| a person (natives prefer *dependable*) | *a **reliable** colleague* ✅ |
| a machine / a vehicle | *a **reliable** engine* ✅ |
| **information** | *a **reliable** source* · ***unreliable** testimony* ✅ |
| **data / a claim** | *that data is **not reliable*** ✅ |
| a promise | *a **reliable** commitment* ⚠️ formal, sounds odd |
| a schedule | *a **reliable** schedule* ⚠️ use *consistent* |
| a person who is not reliable | ❌ *an **unreliable** person* → *someone who **doesn't come through** for you* |

⚠️ **The overlap family, because each of these is a different part of speech:** *reliable* (adj) = you can rely on it · **rely on** (v) = trust it · **reliance** (n) = dependence (*our **reliance** on a single provider is a risk*) · **dependent** (adj) = needing something, and ambiguous about a person · **dependency** (n) = a thing a project needs (*a **dependency** on the API*).
💡 **Cross-domain link — the guarantee levels.** "Reliability" in engineering is a **guarantee about delivery**, and the vocabulary is the same: **at-most-once**, **at-least-once**, **exactly-once**. A colleague who *promises* but flakes is exactly-once semantics failing. See §6.5.

#### 3.13 hard-working — the spelling rule, and a pun

✅ *a **hard-working** student* · *extremely **hard-working*** · *a **hard-working** team*.
❌ **Spelling:** *hardworking* without a hyphen is very common and marked wrong in formal writing. **Use the hyphen.**
⚠️ **Compound-adjective rule:** hyphen only when the word is **before** a noun. *She's **hard-working**.* · *She's very **hard working**.* (adverb *very* cancels the compound — no hyphen.) Same rule for *well-* and *ill-*: *a **well-designed** API* vs *designed **well***.
💡 **Pun worth knowing:** `hardcode` in programming = writing constants into code instead of configuration. A **hard-coded** secret is a real security finding, and the spelling difference from *hard-working* is one hyphen and one letter. Grammar has jokes in it.

#### 3.14 creative — the word every CV abuses

| Word | Sense | Register |
|---|---|---|
| **creative** | producing original ideas | very common; sometimes a cliché in job ads |
| **innovative** | introducing **new methods/practices** | ⭐ tighter and rarer; ⭐ the better CV word |
| **original** | not copied, first of its kind | also: "odd, strange" ❌ |
| **imaginative** | rich, fanciful | *an **imaginative** solution* |
| **inventive** | good at inventing practical fixes | *an **inventive** workaround* |
| **visionary** | seeing the future clearly | ⚠️ often self-applied, sounds arrogant |
| **conventional** | *(the negative)* traditional, unoriginal | ✅ *a **conventional** approach* |

✅ *a **creative** approach* · *the **creative** process* · *think **creatively*** · *a **creative** industry* · ***creative thinking*** · *a **creative** solution to a technical problem*.
⚠️ **Noun trap:** **creativity** is right, ❌ *creativiness* is not. *a burst of **creativity***.
⚠️ **Negative contexts exist:** *a **creative** excuse* · *too **creative** with the requirements* · ***creative accounting*** (inflating profits to hide losses — a real term). A **creative** person who ignores a spec is also a real problem.
★ **The strongest CV form is the verb:** *"I **designed** the caching layer"*, not *"I was **creative**."* An adjective is an opinion; a verb is evidence. A concrete *made* thing beats an adjective about yourself.

#### 3.15 charisma, charismatic — and the leadership theory named after it

| Form | Example |
|---|---|
| **charisma** (n) | *a leader with **charisma*** · *personal **charisma*** · *radiate **charisma*** |
| **charismatic** (adj) | *a **charismatic** speaker* · *charismatic leadership* |
| **charismatically** (adv) | *she spoke **charismatically*** |

✅ *a **charismatic** presence* · *a **charismatic** figure* · *charismatic authority* · *a **charismatic** CEO*.
⚠️ **Not the same as "extroverted."** A charismatic person is *magnetic*, with or without volume. ⚠️ Negative: *a **charismatic** charlatan* — a person with charisma used badly.
💡 **The origin and the theory:** Greek *kharis* "grace, favour" → Latin *carisma* "gift of grace" (Paul's *charismata*, spiritual gifts). **Max Weber's *charismatic authority*** (1919) is a genuine sociology of leadership: legitimacy comes from a leader's perceived extraordinary gift rather than from law or tradition. See §6.4.
⚠️ **Collocations:** not *charismatic with*; use *a **charismatic** speaker* and *a **charismatic** stage presence*.

#### 3.16 devote, devoted, devotee, devotion — the preposition rule

| Form | Sentence |
|---|---|
| **devote** (v) | *She **devoted** her life to medicine.* ✅ |
| | ❌ *She **devoted** medicine.* ← **never** |
| **devoted** (adj) | *a **devoted** friend* · ***devoted to** his family* ✅ |
| **devotee** (n) | *a **devotee** of Plato* · *a **devotee** of Japanese horror* ✅ |
| **devotion** (n) | *with **devotion*** · ***devotion to** the cause* ✅ |
| | *an **ex-devotee** of yoga* ✅ |

✅ *to **devote** time/effort/life/attention + **to** + noun/gerund* — ***devote** your **attention to** the details***, ***devote** myself **to** learning***.
⚠️ **The most common error:** putting the object of devotion after the verb. ❌ *"He **devotes** the project a lot of time"* → ✅ *"He **devotes** a lot of time **to** the project."*
⚠️ *devoted* is a stronger, warmer word than *loyal*: *a **devoted** fan* is a super-fan; *a **loyal** customer* is one who keeps buying. **`devoted` is not a personality trait you can claim about yourself** — it is a thing other people are. Say *"I **devote** myself to continuous learning."*

#### 3.17 The six words that need only a collocation, not an essay

These are on the board and they are genuinely useful, but they have no hidden senses, no irregular suffix and no register trap. What they need is the **right preposition** and the **right complement**, which is exactly what learners get wrong.

| Word | ✅ Correct patterns | ❌ What goes wrong | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **open-minded** | *be **open-minded about** X* · *an **open-minded** approach* · *be **open-minded towards** someone's suggestion* | ❌ *"I'm very open-minded to new ideas"* → preposition is **about / towards** | ↔ **narrow-minded** (the negative); ⚠️ *"open-minded about ignorance"* reads as an accusation |
| **kind** | *be **kind to** someone* · *It is **kind of** you* · *a **kind** gesture* | ❌ *"kind with children"* (Vietnamese *hiền với*) → **to** | *kindness* is the noun; comparative *kinder* — ⚠️ *"kinder than necessary"* means *less aggressive than required*, i.e. **sarcastic** |
| **confident** | *confident **in** yourself* · *confident **about** the outcome* · *confident **that** it will work* | ❌ *confident **of** success* (understandable but **dated** — smells of 1950s textbook) | ⚠️ *"too confident"* = a criticism; the CV-safe version is *"confident in my ability to…"*, which asserts **scope**, not infallibility |
| **patient** | *be **patient with** children* · *be **patient about** delays* · *a **patient** listener* | ❌ *"I'm patient for the results"* | ⭐ **stress on the first syllable** — `PEY-shunt`, not `pa-SHY-unt`; the second-syllable stress makes it sound negative |
| **gentle** / **faithful** | *a **gentle** reminder* · *a **gentle** slope* · ***faithful** to* someone or something | ❌ *"a gentle slope"* needs context — ⚠️ *"gentle on my laptop"* = careful | *gentle* has a **physical** sense (§2.12) and *faithful* has **five** (§2.13) |

💡 **The four prepositions to get right in one line:** `kind **to**` · `patient **with**` · `open-minded **about**` · `loyal/faithful **to**`. All four are preposition errors that make a sentence sound broken rather than merely informal.

### 4. NEGATIVE — the 8 confirmed cells (9 word items), and the words that are not really negative

#### 4.1 arrogant — the biggest cluster on the board

| Word | Shade | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **arrogant** | behaves as if better than others | *an **arrogant** attitude* |
| **conceited** | thinks highly of own abilities | *a **conceited** beginner* |
| **vain** | cares about appearance/own worth | *a **vain** man* |
| **smug** | self-satisfied after winning | *a **smug** "I told you so"* |
| **haughty** | proud, distant, aloof | *a **haughty** manner* |
| **self-important** | thinks too much of own importance | ⚠️ formal criticism |
| **condescending** | talks down to others | ⚠️ the most actionable one |
| **hubristic** | pride before the gods | ⚠️ literary |

✅ *an **arrogant** attitude* · *a **conceited** fool* · *he was **smug** about winning* · *a **haughty** silence* · *a **condescending** tone*.
⚠️ *arrogant* applies to **people, manners, attitudes, and organisations** (⚠️ *"an **arrogant** corporation"* is a common criticism in tech).
⚠️ **It is a behavioural accusation.** If you must say it about a colleague, soften to *"a bit **self-assured**"* or *"comes across as **presumptuous**."*

#### 4.2 selfish — and the field where it is a compliment ⭐

**`selfish` is neutral-to-positive in economics, evolutionary biology, and game theory.** This is the first of the board's words that changes valence by field.

| Field | Meaning | Quote |
|---|---|---|
| everyday | *caring only about yourself* ❌ | *"He's **selfish** — he never shares."* |
| **game theory / economics** | **self-interested** — utility-maximising ✅ | *"We assume agents are **selfish** (rational self-interest)."* |
| **evolution** | genes that propagate themselves ✅ | *the "**selfish** gene" (Dawkins, 1976)* |
| CS (technical) | a program that does not share resources | *a **selfish** scheduling algorithm* (AIMD: Additive Increase Multiplicative Decrease) |

✅ *a **selfish** act* · *a **selfish** choice*.
⚠️ **Don't use it to describe a person in a professional context.** ✅ *"a **self-interested** stakeholder"* is the formal term; *"not a team player"* is the workplace complaint that lands.
★ In an interview, the opposite of *selfish* as an accusation is **`self-interested`** in the strategic sense — so **self-reliance**, **initiative**, and **ambition** are *the same trait as the accusation*, just pointed at a different object. That is the mechanism behind §6.2.

#### 4.3 greedy — and the algorithm named after it ⭐

**`greedy` has three live senses, and the technical one is the most famous in your field:**

| Field | Meaning | Polarity |
|---|---|---|
| everyday | wanting more money/than you need | ❌ |
| **algorithms / DSA** | building a solution by taking the locally best choice at each step | ✅ **neutral — "greedy algorithm"** |
| **LLM sampling** | **greedy decoding** = always take the highest-probability next token | ✅ **neutral — and worse for diversity** |
| ML | **greedy policy / greedy loss** | ✅ neutral |
| networking | **greedy traffic policing** drops packets when a link is full | ✅ neutral |

✅ everyday: *a **greedy** kid* · *a **greedy** corporation* · *too **greedy** to share*.
✅ technical: *a **greedy** algorithm* · *a **greedy** strategy* · ***greedy** versus **dynamic programming*** (Huffman, activity selection, coin change).
⚠️ Note the metaphor: an algorithm is "greedy" because it **takes the biggest available piece at every step** — the same instinct as a person, which is why CS could adopt the word as a compliment. See `dev_foundation/dsa/05-data-structure-selection-guide.md` and §6.5.
⚠️ **Careful in a meeting:** *"let's be **greedy** about scope"* ❌ = take everything. ✅ *"be **aggressive** about scope."*

#### 4.4 rude — the shades, and the two meanings of "rude"

| Word | Shade | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **rude** | impolite, offensive | *a **rude** remark* |
| **impolite** | neutral correction of *rude* | *it was **impolite** of him to interrupt* |
| **disrespectful** | lacks respect | *a **disrespectful** tone* |
| **insolent** | rude **to someone senior** | *an **insolent** junior developer* |
| **brusque** | short and slightly abrupt | *a **brusque** reply* |
| **blunt** | direct, ignoring feelings | *blunt feedback* · *"he's very **blunt**"* |
| **abrasive** | rubs people up the wrong way (person *or* material) | *an **abrasive** personality* · ***abrasive** foam* ✅* |

⚠️ **"Rude" has a second meaning in British English:** *vulgar, obscene* — *"Did he say **rude** words?"* In American English this sense is less common. If you say "he's rude", a BrE listener may briefly think *vulgar*.
⚠️ **Also:** *a **rude** shock* / *a **rude** awakening* (sudden and unpleasant) — not about manners at all.
💡 **The professional version of "rude" — the distinction that matters:**

- *direct* = says what he means → ✅ positive
- *forthright* = says what he means, no beating about the bush → ⚠️ slightly negative
- *blunt* = says what he means, no softening → ⚠️ negative for a person, ✅ positive for a **process**
- *dismissive* = brushes you off → ❌ negative
- *candid* = volunteers the awkward truth → ✅ **the best word in this family**
- *diplomatic* = says what you mean *and* keeps the peace → ✅ **a genuine professional skill**

This is the *courteous + direct* paradox on the board, resolved: in engineering culture, *"I value **blunt** feedback and **candid** reviews"* is a maturity signal, and the Agile spelling of it is ***blameless** post-mortems*.

#### 4.5 lazy — the three-way with idle and inactive ⚠️ + a programming sense

| Word | Core meaning | Typical error |
|---|---|---|
| **lazy** | does not want to work | ❌ *the machine is **lazy*** |
| **idle** | has nothing to do | ❌ *"he is **idle**"* = unemployed, and *that is not a character flaw* |
| **inactive** | not operating / not moving | ✅ *an **inactive** user* |
| **lethargic** | lacking energy | *a **lethargic** Monday morning* |
| **indolent** | lazy, formal/literary | *an **indolent** youth* |

✅ *a **lazy** colleague* · *too **lazy** to check* · *"I'm feeling **lazy** today."* · *a **lazy** afternoon*.
⚠️ **`lazy` in software:** a **lazy** system defers work (lazy loading, lazy evaluation, a lazy list); an **inactive** session has simply expired. *"**Lazy** evaluation is only fast if you don't touch the field."* Both are neutral technical terms.
⚠️ **And the vocabulary of turning features off:** *a **dormant** feature* (exists but disabled) vs *a **deprecated** feature* (scheduled for removal) vs *a **dead** feature*. English has a richer negative-adjective vocabulary for software than it does for people — and for a *deprecation notice* you need exactly this precision.
⚠️ **The most dangerous negative label** — see §6.2: `lazy` attributes a motive (*unwilling*) where `idle` and `blocked` describe a situation. Prefer: *"I was **waiting on** the upstream fix"* over *"I was **lazy**"*.

#### 4.6 cynical — the four-word ladder, and the opposite trap

⚠️ **`cynical`, `jaded`, `sceptical`, and `disenchanted` are all different, and the difference is a timeline.**

| Word | Timeline | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **sceptical** *(skeptical)* | *not yet* | hasn't seen enough evidence | *"I'm **sceptical** about the 40% figure."* |
| **cynical** | *already decided* | believes people act from selfish motives | *"I'm **cynical** about self-reported productivity."* |
| **jaded** | *used to be hopeful* | tired of being disappointed | *"I'm **jaded** after ten years of roadmap promises."* |
| **disenchanted** | *used to believe* | lost faith in something | *"I'm **disenchanted with** the product vision."* |

✅ *be **cynical about** X* · *a **cynical** smile* · *"**too cynical** to try."* · *the **cynicism** of assuming no one acts in good faith*.
⚠️ **The opposite of cynical is not naive** — it is **open / optimistic / trusting**. ❌ *"I was cynical, so I was naive."* And **naive is a criticism in English**, not a compliment — in Vietnamese *ngây thơ* is often gentle; in English *naive* is a warning label.

#### 4.7 cruel — and the cruel/ruthless register flip ⭐

⚠️ **This is the board's best trap: `cruel` is negative in every sense, but `ruthless` is often praise in business and competition.**

| Word | Sense | Business reading |
|---|---|---|
| **cruel** | causing pain, without pity | ❌ **always negative** |
| **heartless** | no compassion at all | ❌ |
| **callous** | indifferent to suffering | ❌ |
| **brutal** | harsh, physical or verbal | ⚠️ *"a **brutal** review"* = accurate, not cruel |

### 6. Cross-Domain Bridge (Language ↔ Psychology ↔ Engineering ↔ Business)

#### 6.1 The board mapped onto the Big Five (OCEAN) ⭐

Personality research did not invent these words; it **measured** them. The five dimensions below (Costa & McCrae, *NEO-PI-R*, 1992 — 5 domains × 6 facets; name from Goldberg's **lexical hypothesis**, 1990: any trait important enough to matter is encoded in ordinary language) place every word on the board into a real, testable framework.

| Domain | What it measures | Words from the board | What it predicts (the useful part) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **O**penness to Experience | novelty, ideas, flexibility of belief | *curious, creative, open-minded, adaptable, imaginative, wise* | learning speed, comfort with new tools, creativity |
| **C**onscientiousness | will the work get done, properly | *diligent, decisive, dependable, responsible, reliable, hard-working, self-reliant, disciplined* | ⭐ **the most consistent predictor of job performance across all roles** (Barrick & Mount 1991; Judge et al. 1997) |
| **E**xtraversion | energy source and social appetite | *outgoing, sociable, energetic, enthusiastic, cheerful, dynamic, bold, charismatic, talkative* | adds predictive power specifically in **sales and management** roles |
| **A**greeableness | how you treat other people | *compassionate, tactful, courteous, kind, gentle, forgiving, trusting, honest, humble, faithful, generous* | teamwork, customer service, low conflict |
| **N**euroticism / Emotionality | reactivity to adversity | *moody, sensitive, anxious, insecure, jealous, suspicious, paranoid, temperamental* | the inverse (**emotional stability**) predicts resilience under stress; high neuroticism predicts burnout and turnover |

**Three findings from this mapping that are worth knowing:**

1. **Nothing on the board is unmeasurable.** Every one of the 66 words is either a facet of one of the five domains or a close neighbour of one. The board's three groups are a *folk taxonomy*; the Big Five is the *factor-analytic* one. The folk taxonomy is easier to remember; the factor-analytic one is what an employer, a researcher, and an assessment tool actually uses.
2. **`stubborn` splits across two domains** — that is why it is hard to place. Unwillingness to change your mind is mostly **low compliance** (an Agreeableness facet) plus **low Openness to change**. The same behaviour reads as *principled* when you agree with it and *obstinate* when you do not, which is a completely different explanation from any of the traits themselves.
3. **The board's "Negative" group is mostly two different things:** Neuroticism (*moody, anxious, insecure, jealous, paranoid* — a **reaction** to circumstances) and low Agreeableness / low Conscientiousness (*arrogant, selfish, greedy, rude, lazy, cynical* — a **choice** about other people). Only the second group is a reliable character judgement; the first is mostly mood, and moods change. That distinction is the entire point of the next section.

#### 6.2 Learned helplessness: why `lazy` is the most dangerous word on the board

Seligman and Maier (1967) found that animals exposed to uncontrollable failure stop trying even when escape later becomes possible. **Learned helplessness** is the human form: a person concludes that effort does not change the outcome, and stops trying.

This matters for a vocabulary note because `lazy` is the word that **asserts a motive** where the situation may deserve the blame:

| What happened | The accurate description | The label |
|---|---|---|
| No clear spec, waited 3 days for a reply | *"I was **blocked on** an unclear requirement."* | *"he was **lazy**"* ❌ |
| Environment gives no feedback on effort | *"the feedback loop was missing"* | *"she doesn't **care**"* ❌ |
| The task was genuinely too hard for the time given | *"the estimate was **unrealistic**"* | *"he's **slow**"* ❌ |

- 📖 Related framework: **locus of control** (Rotter, 1966) — whether you believe outcomes are caused by your own actions (*internal*) or by luck and others (*external*). A **low** internal locus correlates with helplessness; a **high** one is called **self-efficacy** (Bandura, 1977).
- 💡 **The professional move:** a CV adjective exists to **pre-empt a negative label**, and the labels are paired:

| Negative label you must pre-empt | The positive word that pre-empts it |
|---|---|
| *lazy* | **self-starter**, *diligent*, *self-reliant* |
| *arrogant* | **humble**, *candid*, *down-to-earth* |
| *inflexible / stubborn* | **adaptable**, *open-minded* |
| *aloof / unemotional* | **approachable**, *composed*, *sociable* |
| *cynical* | **optimistic**, *pragmatic*, *sceptical* (the *earned* version) |
| *mediocre* | **analytical**, *driven*, *results-oriented* |

⚠️ **Never pre-empt by denying.** ❌ *"I'm **not lazy**"* — the reader now asks why you felt the need to say it. ✅ *"I identify work that isn't assigned — in my last project I proactively instrumented the deployment pipeline."* State the behaviour; the label never gets raised.

#### 6.3 The words that are also ML and psychology terms

| Board word | The field that owns it | What it means there |
|---|---|---|
| **affectionate** | **Affective Computing** (Picard, MIT, 1997) | systems that recognise and respond to human emotion from speech, face, gesture, physiology |
| **sensitive** | signal processing, privacy | a *sensitive* attribute (PII); *sensitive to* noise |
| **impatient / patient** | reinforcement learning | the **exploration–exploitation** trade-off; a *patient* agent keeps sampling |
| **curious** | RL | *curiosity-driven exploration* — reward novelty to escape a local optimum |
| **intuitive** | cognitive science | **System 1** vs System 2 (Kahneman) — fast, confident, and often wrong |
| **stubborn** | optimisation | a *stuck* optimiser: learning rate too high, or local minima |
| **moody / volatile** | time series, markets | a **volatile** market; mood as a *time-varying latent* |

💡 **The career link is direct:** a personality word and its technical namesake are usually the *same metaphor*. A *patient* agent, a *curious* agent, a *sensitive* model, a *stubborn* optimiser — the ML literature borrows the human vocabulary because the behaviours really are analogous. Reading technical papers becomes easier once you know that `sensitive`, `patient`, `volatile`, and `greedy` were never neutral words.

#### 6.4 Charisma, EQ, grit, and the theories named after them

| Concept | Who | What it claims | Board word |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Extraversion / Introversion** | Jung, *Psychological Types* (1921) → **MBTI** (Briggs & Myers) | energy flows inward or outward | *introvert / extrovert* |
| **Charismatic authority** | **Max Weber** (1919) | leadership legitimacy can come from a perceived extraordinary gift, not from law or tradition | *charisma* |
| **Trait = tendency, not behaviour** | APA personality tradition | traits are **inferred** from behaviour over time; they are not directly observable | every adjective on the board |
| **Learned helplessness** | Seligman & Maier (1967) | failure that feels uncontrollable produces passivity | *lazy* (the accusation) |
| **Locus of control / self-efficacy** | Rotter (1966) / Bandura (1977) | do you believe your actions change outcomes? | *self-reliant* |
| **Need for achievement** | McClelland (1961) | a stable drive to excel | *go-getter, ambitious* |
| **Emotional intelligence (EQ)** | Goleman (1995) | self-awareness and regulation, not raw IQ | *unemotional* (the flaw) / *composed* (the skill) |
| **Grit** | Duckworth (2016) | sustained effort over years, not talent | *diligent, hard-working* |
| **Introversion is not a defect** | Cain, *Quiet* (2012) | ~a third of people prefer solitude; deep work is a strength | *quiet, introvert* |
| **HEXACO** | Ashton & Lee | adds **Honesty–Humility** and **Emotionality** to the five | *sincere, humble* (a whole domain the Big Five does not have) |

⚠️ **A caution about MBTI.** It is the most popular of these instruments and the least reliable: test–retest studies find a large fraction of people score a different type on a retest weeks later, which is why serious research uses the Big Five factor analysis instead. The vocabulary is still worth having — IELTS and job interviews both use "introvert/extrovert" — but do not treat a four-letter type as a measurement.

#### 6.5 The same words in an engineering document

Five of the board's "negative" words and three of its "positive" ones are **standard technical terms with no negative valence at all**:

| Word | Technical use | Field | Link |
|---|---|---|---|
| **greedy** | *greedy algorithm* (locally optimal choice), *greedy decoding* (highest-probability token) | DSA, LLM sampling | `dev_foundation/dsa/05-data-structure-selection-guide.md` |
| **lazy** | *lazy loading*, *lazy evaluation* (defer work until needed) | programming | `software_architecture/` |
| **hard-working** ↔ **hardcoded** | `hardcode` = constants baked into code instead of config — a **security finding** | DevSecOps | `git_github/` |
| **reliable** | **at-most-once / at-least-once / exactly-once** delivery guarantees; a *"reliable"* system = one whose guarantees hold under failure | distributed systems | `software_architecture/` |
| **dependable** | a *dependable* service meets its SLO; **D**ependability is an ISO 25010 quality characteristic | SRE / ISO 25010 | `ai_ml/05-mlops-lifecycle-and-deployment-architecture.md` |
| **adaptive** | *adaptive* bitrate, *adaptive* learning rate, *adaptive* interface | ML, networking | `ai_ml/` |
| **dynamic** | a **dynamic** web page renders content per request; the opposite of **static** | web | `dev_foundation/web_basics/` |
| **impersonal** | an **impersonal** system message, de-personalised error copy, anonymised data | UX writing, privacy | `dev_foundation/web_basics/` |

★ **The pattern to internalise:** English technical vocabulary was built by **metaphor from human behaviour**, so the same word carries the human meaning *and* a neutral technical meaning. A **greedy** algorithm is not rude; a **lazy** list is not slack; a **hard-coded** secret is not hardworking. This bilingual life of a word is why technical English feels easier once you know its human root — and why the same knowledge stops you from reading *"relaxed coupling"* as a compliment.

#### 6.6 The register ladder: personality words on paper

The same trait has a different cost at each level of formality. For a CV or LinkedIn post, the middle column is the target.

| Register | How to sound like yourself | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **CV bullet (too plain)** | *good* | *"Good team player"* |
| **CV bullet (the trap)** | adjective about yourself | *"I am a hard-working, responsible and creative person."* |
| **CV bullet (target)** | **verb + evidence** | *"Rebuilt the deployment pipeline, cutting release time from 40 to 6 minutes."* |
| **LinkedIn / public post** | trait + concrete scene | *"I tend to be the person who asks the awkward question in review."* |
| **Cover letter** | trait + professional frame | *"I'm self-reliant in problem-solving and I flag risk early rather than late."* |
| **Interview (spoken)** | trait + contrast | *"I'm quite analytical, though I'm open-minded — I changed my approach after the first user test failed."* |
| **Reference / peer review** | trait + comparison | *"She is the most dependable person on the team — she never dropped a commitment."* |
| **Informal / friend** | plain and warm | *"she's easy-going, you'll like her"* |

⚠️ **Three rules that follow from the table:**
1. **An adjective is an opinion; a verb is evidence.** This is the single highest-leverage edit in any English CV, and it is the same reason IELTS Part 2 rewards *"she is **dependable** — she never missed a stand-up"* over *"she is dependable and hard-working."*
2. **Never pre-empt a negative label by denying it** (§6.2).
3. **Personality adjectives in a CV are almost always a weak signal.** What a hiring manager cannot verify — *hard-working, creative, responsible* — is exactly what nobody believes. What they can verify — *shipped, measured, owned, reduced* — is what carries the weight.

## 📝 Research Journey

- **Why:** A whiteboard recall session on 66 personality adjectives, and the expected payoff was a memorised list. The actual payoff was different: **the board's grouping is a Vietnamese folk taxonomy, and English does not agree with it.** At least six of the 66 words change valence between the board and native usage, and the note's real content became *where* they change and *why*.
- **Struggle:** The hardest part was accepting that a correct dictionary gloss (`nonchalant` = "calm, unbothered") is not the same claim as a correct **usage** — native readers hear something else in most contexts. The second was keeping four registers apart while writing (everyday / business / technical / psychological), and my first draft twice stated a technical sense as if it were the everyday one. The third was resisting the urge to "fix" the board by reclassifying its columns: the board is a record of how Vietnamese sorts these words, and its errors are the most useful data in the note.
- **Aha moment:** The `-ness` engine. Five nouns on the board, and `forgiveness → forgiving` (not *forgiveful*) is a **rule**, not an exception — which means the whole `-ness` vocabulary is generatable from six patterns instead of memorised. The second aha was structural: the board's two Positive columns are **character vs temperament**, which is why they feel so different; and that distinction matters because only the first column carries moral weight in English. The third was that `greedy algorithm`, `lazy loading`, and `reliable` delivery are the *same three words* the board marks negative — the folk taxonomy has simply never met engineering.
- **Career link:** Two concrete payoffs. First, a CV and a public LinkedIn post are **written** English, where every personality adjective carries an implied verdict — so the choice of *self-starter* over *not lazy* is a credibility decision, not a style preference. Second, the mapping onto the Big Five gives the language a measurable backbone: Conscientiousness is the trait that most consistently predicts job performance, and the words in Column 1 map almost exactly onto it. Being able to say *"I'm self-reliant and dependable — I flag risk early"* is the language version of the trait the evidence says matters.
