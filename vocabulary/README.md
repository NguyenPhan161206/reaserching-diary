# Vocabulary & Academic Collocation Hub

## Purpose
This folder is dedicated to advanced vocabulary acquisition, technical collocations, word families, and Viet-Glish corrections tailored for an AI Engineer student (Phan Hữu Bình Nguyên). 

Each note in this directory extracts, deep-dives, and contextualizes terminology from AI/ML, IoT, software engineering, and business economics, complete with system architecture mapping and market pain-point analysis where applicable.

### Progression
The first four notes build core domain vocabulary. **05–12 are all single-mechanism deep dives** — one prefix, one verb family, one near-synonym cluster at a time, which is how accuracy is actually built. **13 returns to a whiteboard-sized list** and applies the same mechanisms across the whole set at once, then bridges into psychology (Big Five, learned helplessness) and engineering (greedy algorithms, lazy loading, reliable delivery). **14 turns note 13's register ladder into a working checklist** — a search list to run before submitting anything written, with the formats where adjectives *are* correct and the twelve words whose meaning shifts rather than merely sounding weak.

**15 is the English-side counterpart to 12.** Where note 12 starts from the Vietnamese word (*tận dụng*) and asks which of nine English words to pick, note 15 starts from three English verbs and asks what each one actually does — its literal root, its borrowed technical sense, and its register. It is also the third note in the run (13, 14, 15) to treat **register** as the real subject rather than meaning: *harness* needs a force, *utilize* is a dashboard word and an inflated verb, and *compress* only means "summarise" by metaphor. Like note 14 and unlike note 13, note 15 deliberately has **no practice twin** — the *utilize* correction has to land while drafting, not in a speaking drill.

**16 returns to the Vietnamese-source-term format of note 12** — one Vietnamese word, several English nouns — but picks the single most over-applied noun in student technical English: *"quá trình"*. Its organising idea is a **structural** split rather than a register one: a **process** is a transformation with an input and an output, a **procedure** is a rule-bound *how*, a **pipeline** is static stages flowing one direction, a **lifecycle** is one stateful entity's states over time, a **journey** is a human narrative, and a **progression** is a position on a ladder. The two most useful consequences: *"learning process"* is a translation artefact where English says *learning journey*, and *"in the process of working"* is an idiom failure where English says *in the course of*. Like notes 14 and 15, it has **no practice twin** — the discrimination has to land at the moment of writing.

**17 and 18 break the one-source-term mould.** Both were requested as raw **batches of English words** (not a Vietnamese source term), each batch belonging to one IELTS passage — a city trying to price cars out of its centre while preserving an ancient *souk*, and a modern-architecture exhibition on the one hand; a sustainable-fashion passage on the other. The shared lesson is **the specialist sense hidden inside a plain word**: *construction* also means "an interpretation" (*a social construction*), *expose* in a building means "leave deliberately visible" (not "endanger"), *capture* means "reproduce faithfully" (*capture light*), *monitor* is a *verb* the city performs continuously, and *track* means "follow over time". Note **17** is the built-environment note (urban policy + materials + Modernism's *flat roof* and *exposed concrete*), and its bridge is the **Pigouvian tax**: a traffic fee is a price on the negative externality the exhaust fumes impose, which is why the city must *monitor* before it *regulates*. Note **18** is the market/ESG note, where the traps are **false friends** — *durable* (a quality) vs *lasting* (a duration), *appeal* (*attract* vs *formally request*, different prepositions), *conscious* (aware) vs *conscientious* (diligent — note 13's Big Five trait), and *concern* (worry) vs *concerned* (worried *or* involved, where word order is meaning). Its bridge separates **stated** from **revealed** preference and **marketing claim** from **verifiable metric** — the reason *sustainability* and *traceability* are the same data problem. Both carry no practice twin, consistent with 14–16.

**20 moves the folder into a new domain without leaving the Template V method.** It takes a single Vietnamese–English keyword list for UAV operations in bad weather and sorts it onto the three-layer map already used in `iot_aiot/06` — *fly* (gust rejection, DOBC, MPC, DRL, PINN), *see* (DVE, sensor fusion, dehazing/deraining/desnowing, low-visibility SLAM), *decide* (weather-aware routing, energy-aware routing, dynamic geofencing, microclimate, onboard meteorology, icing diagnostics, fail-safe landing/RTH). The vocabulary lesson is that **the Vietnamese gloss collapses distinct engineering objects**: "triệt tiêu" covers *reject / cancel / eliminate*, "bền vững" covers *robust* (control) and *sustainable* (ESG), "tư thế" covers *attitude* and *posture*, and "sương mù" covers *fog* (water droplets) and *haze* (dry aerosol). The note carries a cross-cutting distinction table, the formulas a candidate should be able to state (wind triangle, MPC objective, haze model, disturbance observer), and a starting-reference table. Unlike notes 14–19 it is explicitly an **anchor**: the concept lives here, the executable work belongs to the UAV project repositories.

**Note 13 is the first note here with a cross-repo twin:** the same whiteboard exists as a Vietnamese-language Speaking lesson in `ielts-language-lap` (`SP-personality-traits-vocab.md`). Note 14 deliberately has **no** twin — its corrections have to land at the moment of drafting, not in a speaking drill.
